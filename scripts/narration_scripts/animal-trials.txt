This article is about something that sounds like a joke. In the past, animals were put on trial in court, just like people.

It is not only a story from long ago. In 2008, a bear in North Macedonia was found guilty of stealing honey from a beekeeper. The bear ran away before anyone could catch it. The beekeeper was paid by the state.

But such cases were once quite common. From the late Middle Ages until well into the age of reason, courts in many European countries put animals on trial. One historian made a list of nearly two hundred cases. The animals in the courtroom were dogs, horses, roosters and pigs.

Pigs got into trouble most often. They will eat almost anything, so they were often blamed when a small child was hurt. In one French city, a pig was dressed in a jacket and gloves and brought to court. It was found guilty and punished. In another town, a pig was blamed for the death of a five-year-old boy.

Why did people do this? Many of us think it was because people in the Middle Ages believed in strange things. But that is probably wrong. The trials went on long after the Middle Ages had ended. They also happened in different countries, with different beliefs.

One historian has a different idea. He says these trials were about showing that the law was strong. In hard and frightening times, people wanted to see the leaders keeping order. A trial, even of an animal, made it look as if the law was working.

There were also trials by the Church. In these, officials took pests like mice and locusts to court. The animals even got a lawyer. They were told three times to come to court. Of course, they never came. And then they were thrown out of the Church.

An economist thinks these trials were also a message to the people. They showed that the Church's words had real power. That helped the Church to keep getting its money.

So an animal trial looks silly to us. But it may have had a clear purpose. That is why the trials lasted so long.
