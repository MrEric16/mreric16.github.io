This article is about teenagers and sleep. Many parents say it is a daily fight to get a teenager out of bed for school. The writer, who has studied this for twenty years, says it is not about attitude. It is about biology.

Here is how it works. When a person becomes a teenager, something changes in the brain. The body clock moves later. This was found by scientists in the 1980s. It happens to almost all people, and also to most mammals.

The key is a hormone called melatonin. It makes us sleepy. In teenagers, the brain starts to make it at about a quarter to eleven at night. It keeps making it until about eight in the morning. So a teen cannot easily fall asleep before that time. And a teen cannot easily wake up before it ends.

This is not a choice. The writer talked to hundreds of teenagers. They said that when they went to bed early, they just stared at the ceiling until about a quarter to eleven. So telling teens to go to bed earlier does not fix the problem.

Younger children are different. Their body clocks wake them up early, so early school fits them well. That is why it made sense long ago, when older students went to school first. But for teenagers, it does not fit.

How much sleep do teens need? Experts say eight to ten hours a night. That means the earliest healthy time to wake up is about seven in the morning. And a study found that it takes teens, on average, fifty-four minutes from waking up to leaving the house. Yet nearly half of high schools in the United States start before eight. Most start before eight thirty.

What happens when teens do not sleep enough? Their mood gets worse. In one study, feelings of sadness went from about one in five teenagers to about half, among teens who slept four hours or less. Car accidents are also more common among tired teenagers.

The good news is that schools that moved to a later start have seen real benefits. In one town in Wyoming, car crashes among teenagers dropped by seventy percent in the first year. Students' marks also improved. The first school district to change was in Minnesota, in the 1990s. Now hundreds of schools in forty-four states have done it. Big health groups say high schools should start at eight thirty or later.

Still, many schools say no. They worry about buses, sports and the timing of other grades. But schools that did change found answers. Some used buses with mixed ages. Some worked with city transport. Some offered more after-school care.

The writer's message is simple. If we really care about our children, we should be ready to solve these problems.
