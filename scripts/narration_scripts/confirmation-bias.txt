This article talks about confirmation bias — the habit our brains have of noticing and trusting information that agrees with what we already believe, while brushing past anything that doesn't.

Here's the basic idea. Imagine two people look at the exact same piece of evidence. If it matches what they already think, they'll call it strong and convincing. If it goes against what they already think, they'll find reasons to doubt it, question the source, or just forget about it faster. Same evidence, two completely different reactions — and the only real difference is what each person believed walking in.

Why do our brains work this way? There are a few reasons. First, thinking carefully takes real mental effort, and our brains are always looking for shortcuts. Simply accepting information that fits what we already believe is much easier than stopping to check it properly. Second, some scientists think this habit comes from survival instincts. Early humans needed to react fast to danger, not slowly weigh every possible explanation, so quick pattern-matching won out over careful analysis. Third, and maybe most important, it protects our feelings. Being told we're wrong about something we care about is uncomfortable. Looking for evidence that we're right, instead, feels a lot better — even if it isn't accurate.

Scientists describe confirmation bias as working in three separate stages. First, we tend to avoid information that challenges our beliefs in the first place — this is called selective exposure. Second, when we can't avoid it, we twist how we interpret it, so it somehow still supports what we already thought — this is selective perception. Third, afterward, we remember the parts that agreed with us much better than the parts that didn't — this is selective retention.

There's a well-known experiment from the late 1970s that shows this clearly. Researchers gave university students two made-up studies about the death penalty — one study seemed to show it worked, the other seemed to show it didn't. No matter which side a student already supported before reading, they ended up more confident in their original opinion afterward. Both groups used the exact same evidence to become more certain they were right from the start.

Confirmation bias affects how people judge each other too — in hiring, an interviewer who forms an early opinion about a candidate may, without realizing it, interpret the rest of the interview in a way that backs up that early opinion, fair or not.

The internet has made this bias even stronger. Search engines and social media predict what each person wants to see, based on past clicks. One well-known example happened after a major oil spill years ago: two different people, searching the exact same company name, got completely different results — one saw investment pages, the other saw articles about environmental damage — because the algorithm had already guessed what each person was more likely to click on. This is called a filter bubble.

On a larger scale, this same pattern builds echo chambers — online spaces where almost everyone already agrees with each other. Inside an echo chamber, a belief tends to grow more extreme over time, because it's constantly confirmed and never seriously challenged.

So what helps? Just knowing confirmation bias exists is a real first step. Beyond that, starting with neutral facts from several reliable sources, actually listening to people who disagree, and noticing when a piece of information makes you defensive — that discomfort is often a clue it's worth taking seriously, not dismissing.
