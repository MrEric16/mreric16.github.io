This article is about the walls of Pompeii. Two thousand years ago, people there wrote on them all the time. And it sounds a lot like the internet.

First, a quick reminder. Pompeii was a busy Roman city. In the year 79, a nearby volcano blew up. Ash and rock fell on the city for hours. By the next night, Pompeii was buried under about six metres of it.

People first started digging it up about three hundred years ago. They found art, houses and shops. At first, the experts only cared about the big, beautiful things. They said the scribbles on the walls were boring and not important. They were wrong. Some of the best finds are small. They are words on walls.

Experts have counted more than eleven thousand of them in Pompeii. Shopkeepers wrote them. Workers wrote them. Soldiers wrote them. Even enslaved people wrote them. Some are quick scribbles. Some are neat, with real skill. Kids drew gladiators they saw fight in the arena. The drawings are rough, just like a kid's drawing today.

What did people write about? Almost everything. They said hello to friends. They wrote love notes. They wrote jokes, poems and insults. They wrote reviews of places to eat. One building near the arena has more than four hundred messages. It was the most popular spot for writing.

Some walls look like a comment thread. Two brothers left notes for each other on a friend's wall. On one staircase, people added their own funny lines to the same short poem, one after another. Almost everyone in the city could read. So a wall could be a place to talk. Men wrote more than women. But many notes were written to women, so women were meant to read them. And some women wrote back.

That is why a writer who studies this says the graffiti is like social media. It is not the great speeches of famous people. It is ordinary people, talking about ordinary things. And that is exactly why it is so useful. It makes the Romans feel real. They were not just statues in a museum. They were people like us, with friends, crushes and enemies.

There was also politics. Professional painters wrote big notices to support people in an election. They said a person was honest and good with money. They promised free bread and free shows. Sometimes they attacked a rival. They said he was friends with drunks and thieves. These notices were not scribbled. They were painted neatly by people who did this as a job. So the Romans really did argue about how their city should be run, just like we do.

And people loved to brag. One man wrote that a gladiator was the idol of all the girls. He was probably the gladiator! Two men even fought in writing over a woman who worked in a bar. They called each other names on the wall.

So we should be careful. Graffiti is not always true. It is only what one person wanted to say. A person who writes on a wall about how great he is may just be showing off. Experts have to think about who wrote it, and why.

Still, the messages tell us what people cared about. They loved love, sport, food, fame and a good fight. Some experts say the gladiators were like the football stars of today. The poems were like the songs we share. People wanted to be part of something and to be noticed. That has not changed in two thousand years.

But there is a favourite joke. Many walls say the same thing. They say, "Wall, I am surprised you have not fallen down. You carry so many scribbles."

The tools are different now. We use phones, not sharp sticks. But the need is the same. People want to talk, to joke, to be seen and to feel close to others. That is why this old graffiti feels so close to us today.
