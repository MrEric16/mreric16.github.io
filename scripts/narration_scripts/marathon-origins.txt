This article talks about where the marathon race actually came from — and most of what people believe about it is a myth.

The famous story goes like this. In the year 490 B.C., a huge battle happened near a place called Marathon, in Greece. The Greeks fought against a much bigger Persian army, and somehow, they won. According to the legend, a messenger named Pheidippides ran all the way from Marathon to the city of Athens, about 26 miles, just to deliver the good news. The story says that as soon as he arrived and shouted that they had won, he collapsed on the ground and died from exhaustion.

It's a dramatic story. But it's probably not true — or at least, not in that exact form. There really was a runner named Pheidippides, and he really did something incredible. But it wasn't running to Athens with news of victory. According to an old Greek historian, before the battle even happened, Pheidippides ran about 150 miles in under two days, to a different city called Sparta, to ask for soldiers to help fight the Persians. That's the real, documented achievement — a much longer and arguably more impressive run than the famous 26-mile story. As for who actually brought the victory news back to Athens after the battle, historians think it was probably a different messenger, with a different name entirely. The dramatic version — Pheidippides running, shouting the news, and dying — doesn't even show up in writing until a poem written almost 2,400 years after the real events. In other words, the most famous part of the whole story was likely invented by a poet, centuries later, not recorded by anyone who was actually there.

So if the legend isn't where the marathon race came from, where did it actually start? The answer is much more recent than most people expect: 1896. That year, the Olympic Games were brought back to life after being gone for over a thousand years. A scholar helping organize the new Olympics suggested adding a long-distance running race that would feel connected to ancient Greek history, even though the "true" history behind it wasn't quite what people assumed. That very first modern marathon covered about 25 miles, roughly following the old route from Marathon toward Athens, and a Greek runner ended up winning it — a popular, almost perfect result for the host country.

And here's maybe the strangest part of the whole story: the exact distance we use today, 26.2 miles, wasn't even decided until 1908, twelve years later, in London. The reason behind that specific number has nothing to do with ancient history at all. Olympic organizers simply extended the race route by a little extra distance, so that it would finish directly in front of the royal family's viewing box, letting the king and queen watch the runners cross the finish line up close. That extra stretch of road, added basically for the royal family's convenience, became the new official distance — and it's remained exactly 26.2 miles in every marathon run anywhere in the world ever since.
