This article is about GeoCities. It was a website where millions of people built their first home page. It started in the nineteen-nineties, a very different time on the internet.

Back then, people connected with slow dial-up modems. Most users only looked at things. They did not make them. To make a web page, you needed to know a coding language called HTML, and very few people did.

One man saw it differently. He thought the web was like a new land. People would want to claim a piece of it and build a home there. So he offered pieces of the web for free. He grouped the sites into neighbourhoods by subject. He also gave people templates, to make learning the basics easy. The service opened in nineteen ninety-four, and it was called GeoCities.

For the next fifteen years, users built more than thirty-eight million pages. Many of them were a mix of simple pictures, music files that played by themselves, and flashing letters. There were lots of toys to play with. Most people did not worry if the colours and parts matched. The writer says it was as if someone's decorated school folder had been put on the internet.

The pages were about anything. Pets, movies, television, places, and tributes to relatives who had died. There were fan pages for actors, and personal pages that just said hello. Users were called homesteaders. And pages were linked to others on similar topics. If you liked a page about someone's Persian cat, GeoCities could show you more cat pages. This was before search engines were part of everyday life. So those links helped people to find their way around.

There were no likes. Instead, each page had a counter, which showed how many people had visited. Most owners had an email address on the site. And in those days, a message from a stranger in Brazil or Iceland was exciting.

By nineteen ninety-eight, two million people had joined. Each one got fifteen megabytes of space for pages, photos and simple music. The site made money from adverts. It had nineteen million visitors a month. Only two other sites were bigger.

But some creators were not happy. The site placed a watermark at the bottom of every page, which slowed down loading on dial-up. Some also felt it was odd that GeoCities owned their material.

In nineteen ninety-nine, a big search company bought it for three point six billion dollars. But the adverts did not make as much money as hoped. And things were changing. In two thousand three, a social network appeared where people could share their thoughts with a ready audience. On GeoCities, you had to hope that someone would find your page.

In two thousand nine, the company decided to close it down, and delete all the pages. Some people thought it was not a big loss. But internet historians disagreed. They wanted to keep these records of early internet culture.
