This article is about money. Today, money is paper, coins and numbers on a phone. But in the past, people used much stranger things. Here are some of them.

Teeth. In the Pacific islands, people used the teeth of animals as wealth. In Fiji, in the nineteen hundreds, the most valuable item was a polished tooth from a sperm whale. In other islands, people prized the teeth of dogs, pigs and even dolphins. They used them to buy canoes and food. They even used them to make peace between groups.

Cheese. In Italy, it was more than a joke that cheese is like gold. By the thirteen hundreds, a famous hard cheese had become a kind of investment. Even today, an Italian bank takes big wheels of this cheese as a promise for a loan. It stores millions of dollars' worth of cheese in cool, safe rooms.

Shells. People in many parts of the world, from North America to West Africa, once used shells as money. Some Native American groups made beads from sea shells and used them to buy things and to make agreements. In Africa and Asia, small sea snail shells called cowries were the main money for a very long time. They were used in some places into the nineteen hundreds. The American slang word clams, for dollars, comes from this shell money.

Tea. In Central and Northern Asia, people used blocks of tea. They were made from dried leaves in China, pressed into bricks. In places like Tibet, Mongolia and Siberia, they were the best money. They were strong, easy to carry, and you could drink them. Traders on the Silk Road loved them. The bricks had marks to show their quality. Some had pictures cut into them, to prove they were real. In the early nineteen hundreds, travellers found that tea could buy more than gold or silver.

Salt. In old Ethiopia, salt was so important to survive that people called it white gold. It was cut into flat bars. Traders in the desert valued these bars highly. They used them for payment and trade, and even for food. We still say that someone is worth their salt. The word comes from an old Roman custom. The Roman soldiers' pay was a salt allowance. The word salary comes from this.

So money is whatever people agree to value. It does not always have to be printed by a government.
