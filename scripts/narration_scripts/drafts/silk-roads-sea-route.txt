This article tells the story of how ancient Rome and China traded with each other, and how a lost sailor helped open a sea route to India.

Many people picture the Silk Road as a single path across the desert. In fact, it was a web of many roads, on land and on sea. And the sea part is a fascinating story.

Let us start with a meeting that almost went wrong. About eighteen hundred years ago, officials from the Roman emperor arrived at the Chinese court. They had travelled by ship around Southeast Asia and then walked about twelve hundred miles inland. The Chinese had known for a long time that the Roman Empire existed. They saw it as a great power, almost as big as their own. This was the first direct contact. But when the visitors arrived, the court was not impressed. They had brought only small things like ivory, horns and shells that they had picked up on the way, nothing that showed the glory of Rome. The Chinese even wondered if the men were just merchants pretending to be diplomats. They were also puzzled that the group had come by the southern route. The normal way was overland through Central Asia.

Why did Rome want to reach China at all? Because silk had become the height of fashion. Rich Romans loved it, and traders were willing to travel across the world to get it.

Now for the sailor. The story says that a ship in the Red Sea found a boat drifting without a crew, except for one man who was nearly dead. Nobody understood his language, so they took him to Alexandria, the great port city of Egypt. There he learned Greek. He was from India, and he said his boat had been blown off course. He was grateful for the care he had received, so he offered to guide any ship back to his homeland.

The king sent an expedition with a Greek explorer in charge. The sailor taught them the key secret. The wind in the Indian Ocean changes with the seasons. From March to September, the monsoon blows toward India. From October to February, it blows back toward Egypt. If you use the wind correctly, the journey takes just weeks instead of months. The explorer came home with spices and jewels, and the sea route was open.

After Egypt became part of the Roman Empire, Alexandria turned into the main door for goods from the East. Ships brought pearls, spices, ivory and silk to ports on the Red Sea. Then camels carried the goods across the desert, mostly at night to avoid the heat, to the River Nile. From there, boats took them to Alexandria. At the busiest port, up to one hundred and twenty ships left for India every year.

The result was a mix of cultures. In Alexandria, you could meet people from Syria, Arabia, Persia and India, all listening to the same lectures. A scrap of old paper found in Egypt even contains a comic play set in India, although it shows that people made fun of foreigners then, as they sometimes do now. In an Indian port, there were so many foreign traders from Rome that they built a temple to the first Roman emperor.

The lesson of the Silk Roads is that trade carries more than goods. Along with silk and spices, people exchanged ideas, beliefs and stories. And one rescued sailor, a changing wind, and a clever explorer changed history.
