This article is about a long-time mystery. How do fireflies make light?

Scientists have known some facts about firefly light for about sixty years. They knew what goes into the firefly's glowing body. It needs oxygen, calcium, magnesium, and a natural chemical called luciferin. And they knew what comes out. It is light, in yellow, green, orange and sometimes even blue flashes that you see on summer nights. But what happens in between was a mystery. Think of the firefly's body as a black box.

There was also a problem. The usual idea did not work. Oxygen and luciferin do not normally react with each other in the way they would need to make light. A chemist explains it like this. Apples usually mix only with apples. Oranges mix only with oranges. And oxygen and luciferin are like an apple and an orange.

Now a team of scientists has found the answer. The oxygen in the firefly's body is a special kind. It carries one extra electron. This kind of oxygen can act like both an apple and an orange. So it can react with luciferin, and make light.

The scientist who led the work thinks that this may be how all glowing animals work, from tiny plankton to deep-sea fish.

And there is a practical use. Luciferin is already used in medicine. Doctors use it to take pictures of tumours. It also helps to develop medicines that fight cancer. In another study, scientists used it to find certain chemicals in the brains of living rats. One day, this may give doctors a new way to look at the human brain.

But the lead scientist says the main reason is simple. They just want to know how nature works. The uses may or may not follow.
