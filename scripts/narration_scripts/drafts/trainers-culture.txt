This article tells how trainers, or sneakers, went from sports shoes to a huge world culture.

Sneakers were first made in England in the eighteen-sixties, for rich people playing croquet and tennis. For a long time, people wore them for sport, not for fashion. Today they are a whole culture. They are a way to show who you are, and they appear in museums and auction houses, where one pair can sell for millions of dollars. The business is now worth about seventy-nine billion dollars.

Most collectors, called sneakerheads, say the story began in the late nineteen-seventies and early eighties, when famous athletes began to put their names on shoes. For decades, one canvas basketball shoe had ruled the courts, and then other brands, like Puma and Adidas, joined in. An expert at a shoe museum in Toronto says that in New York, basketball, hip-hop and break dancing were all mixing together.

But the real change came in nineteen eighty-five, with the Air Jordan One. In nineteen eighty-four, Michael Jordan was a talented newcomer who had not yet played a professional game. Nike, then better known for running shoes, saw him as its future and gave him a five-year deal worth two and a half million dollars. The shoes came in bold white, black and red. The basketball league had a rule that shoes must be at least fifty-one percent white, so the shoes were a kind of challenge. Each time he wore them, he was fined five thousand dollars. Nike simply paid the fine. It was a clever bet. Jordan became one of the best players ever, and the shoes became hugely popular.

Soon sneakers moved beyond basketball. In nineteen eighty-six, a famous hip-hop group released a song about their Adidas shoes, and got the first deal of its kind with the company. A little later, a rock singer from the band Nirvana made another brand a symbol of youth. At the same time, many offices introduced casual Fridays. Men could put away their suits and wear something that showed who they really were.

As demand grew, the companies made even more excitement. They teamed up with celebrities and luxury brands, and released small numbers of special shoes. Rare pairs became wanted by collectors, and a market for reselling appeared. The collector mind is simple. If you have the first, second, third and fifth, you must find the fourth. When resellers raised the prices very high, it only made the shoes seem more special.

Famous singers and rappers shaped the shoe world for almost ten years with their own designs. Later, reality TV stars wore them too. This brought in a whole new group of people. It mixed high fashion with street fashion, which the shoe business had not seen before.

By the middle of the twenty-tens, sneakers were status symbols. In twenty sixteen, a rapper ordered a pair of Air Jordans covered in twenty-four carat gold. They cost an estimated two point one million dollars, and each shoe weighed fifty pounds. A professor in New York says that wearing rare shoes became a way to show your place in society, and to say you are a little better than others.

But not every collector is like that. One expert says the stereotype is that we are all in it for excess and status. It is a part of the culture, she says, but not the whole thing. A young man in Florida has about a hundred pairs. For him, the best part is the community. He met true friends by queuing all night for new releases, and now they share a group chat. Others see sneakers as art. A designer in Baltimore turns shoes into wearable artworks. He says that the first thing you do is admire what you see.

Today, more than one hundred million pairs of the Air Jordan One have been sold. In April twenty twenty-three, a pair that Jordan wore in his final season sold for two point two million dollars. That is the most ever paid for sneakers at auction. One expert notes that nearly forty years later, the company still sells the same shoe. Because of that, every child dreams of making a shoe line.

Sneaker culture has a strong history of storytelling, she says. And everyone wants to tell their story.
