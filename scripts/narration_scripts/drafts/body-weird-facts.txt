This article shares weird facts about the normal human body, which can be stranger than fiction.

Start with your tongue. It has something in common with an octopus's arm and an elephant's trunk. All three are called muscular hydrostats, which means a bundle of muscles that work without any bones. They are mostly water, so their volume stays the same. When some cells squeeze, the whole thing has to get bigger somewhere else. This makes them strong and flexible. The article jokes that if your tongue were as big as an elephant's trunk, it could pull up trees.

Next, a small bone shaped like a horseshoe. It is called the hyoid bone, and it sits at the top of your throat. It is not connected to any other bone. Its job is to anchor the muscles of the mouth floor and the tongue.

The little dent under your nose has a name too. It is called the philtrum. It does nothing useful. It is only a reminder of your time before birth, when the two sides of your face grow separately, then join in the middle. The old Romans named the dip in the upper lip Cupid's bow. The word philtrum comes from a Greek word meaning love potion.

Now hair. A popular story says that hair keeps growing after you die. This is false. The skin simply shrinks as it dries, and that creates the illusion of growth. In fact, hair is both living and dead. A living follicle pushes it out, but the hair itself is made of dead cells filled with keratin. This is the same material as the top layer of your skin and your nails. When hair turns grey, it means the colour cells in it are dying.

Have you noticed that toenails grow more slowly than fingernails? The outer bones of your toes are shorter than those in your fingertips, and nails grow at a rate that follows the length of those bones. In the same way, the nail on your middle finger grows faster than the one on your little finger. It has to do with humans needing claws less and less.

Here is a surprising one. Humans glow. Fireflies and jellyfish glow, and so do we, as a natural result of our body chemistry. But it was only in two thousand and nine that researchers in Japan built a camera a thousand times more sensitive than the human eye, and filmed it. The light seems to follow a twenty-four hour cycle. It is brightest in the late afternoon, and on the cheeks, forehead and neck. So if someone says you are glowing, it could be true.

Inside your cells, there is a tiny protein called kinesin. It carries important molecules to where they are needed. The strange part is how it travels. It walks along a tiny path, using two parts at its base that scientists call feet.

Then there is a protein in the brain that scientists named after a famous video game hedgehog. In nineteen ninety-three, researchers found it in fruit flies, where it made spines grow on the back. So they gave it a fun name, and other similar ones are named after hedgehogs too.

The liver, too, deserves respect. It may look lumpy and awkward, but it cleans your body, and it is perhaps the most resilient of the big organs. It can grow back from only a quarter of its tissue. You might not call it the cutest, but it might be most likely to succeed.

Finally, the body keeps some leftovers from evolution. You may know about the tailbone, the appendix and even the little toe. But there is also a tiny organ inside the nose. In animals, it helps to sense chemical signals in the air. In humans, it seems to do almost nothing, and it is not even connected to the brain by nerves. Scientists still argue about whether it does anything at all.

So your body is full of surprises, even the normal parts.
