This article is about the first time scientists looked inside the brain of a moving octopus. They found something nobody had seen before.

Octopuses are famous for being clever. They have great memories. They are masters of camouflage. They are curious, and they use tools to solve problems. Some scientists even think they dream. When an octopus sleeps, waves of colour move across its skin. People have been fascinated by them for almost two thousand years. An ancient Roman writer noticed that they had a kind of mischief and cunning.

But it is very hard to study their brains. An octopus has no bones. Its arms can reach every part of its body. So it can easily grab anything stuck to it and pull it off. And there is no hard place where you can fix a device.

The scientists in this study found a way. They worked with three octopuses in tanks. They put a very light recording device under the skin of each octopus, between its eyes. It is the kind of device used on birds. Then they connected it to wires in the part of the brain that is used for learning and memory. For twelve hours, they recorded the brain while the octopuses slept, cleaned themselves and explored their tank. This had never been done before in animals that move freely.

The results were a surprise. First, they found brain waves similar to those in the memory centre of the human brain. This is strange. Octopuses and humans have not had a common ancestor for about seven hundred and fifty million years. That ancestor was a small flat worm that lived on the sea floor. It had almost no brain. So two different animals developed the same kind of signal by themselves. Scientists call this convergent evolution. They also found waves that control sleeping and waking in other animals.

Then they found something completely new. It was a long, slow wave that repeated two times every second. No one has seen this in any other animal. Nobody knows what it does.

To find out, scientists need more recordings. They plan to train octopuses to do certain tasks, and then watch the brain at the same time. By repeating the same behaviour many times, they can match patterns in the brain with actions. This is the key method used in animals with backbones.

So we have our first small look inside an octopus mind. And it already shows that it is both like ours and very different.
