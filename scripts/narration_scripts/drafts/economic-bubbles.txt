This article tells the stories of six famous economic bubbles, times when prices rose madly, and then crashed.

The first is about flowers. In the sixteen-thirties, people in Holland fell in love with tulips. They became a status symbol. Rich people and ordinary people alike rushed to buy rare ones. Tulip markets opened in the city centres, and bulbs were traded like shares on a modern stock market. One single bulb could cost as much as a carriage and two horses. In February sixteen thirty-seven, a few big sellers decided to cash in. Prices fell and panic followed. Some rich traders were left almost as beggars.

The second happened in Britain in the early seventeen-hundreds. This is where the word bubble comes from. A trading firm called the South Sea Company took over a huge part of the country's war debt, in return for the right to trade with South America. It earned very little. But the owners spread rumours of great riches, and sold new shares. People could pay in small parts, so everyone wanted to join in. The price rose from about one hundred and twenty-eight pounds in January to nearly one thousand by June. Then everybody started selling. By December, the price was back to a hundred and twenty-four. Thousands were ruined, and later some politicians were arrested for taking bribes.

The third is from France, at the same time. The country had big debts and too little money. A Scottish gambler and money expert named John Law suggested using paper money. He started a bank and a trading company that had the right to trade in the French lands in North America, where people believed there was gold. The price of its shares jumped from five hundred to eighteen thousand in less than a year. But no riches were ever found, and the bank printed too much paper money. Prices shot up. In seventeen-twenty, people tried to swap their paper for gold and found there was only enough for a small part of it. Banks were emptied, and many new millionaires became poor overnight. Law had to run away in disguise, afraid for his life.

The fourth was in Florida in the nineteen-twenties. After the First World War, many Americans went south looking for cheap land and an easy life. Prices doubled every few months. Some buyers paid only a small deposit and sold the land again before they had to pay the rest. Cheats moved in too. One sold plots he said were in a big city, but they were really in a swamp, sixty-five miles away. In early nineteen twenty-six, building materials and buyers ran out. Then a hurricane hit that autumn. In three years, the money going through the state's banks fell from over one billion dollars to under one hundred and fifty million.

The fifth is the railway boom in Britain in the eighteen-forties. Hundreds of new lines were planned in just a few years, and Parliament approved plans for more than nine thousand miles of track. At one point, the money spent on railways was more than twice what the government spent on its army. But railway shares peaked in eighteen forty-five, then slid for years. A famous woman novelist saw her shares fall from one hundred and twenty pounds to just twenty. She wrote that many people had almost lost their daily bread.

The last is the most famous. In the Roaring Twenties, American share prices more than quadrupled. Ordinary people borrowed money to buy shares. Banks even used customers' money without asking. Some warned of a fall, but many respected experts said the good times would last. On twenty-fourth October nineteen twenty-nine, called Black Thursday, shares crashed. People sold so fast that the machines could not keep up, with about thirteen million trades in one day. By nineteen thirty-three, about four thousand banks had failed. This helped to bring on the Great Depression, which lasted around ten years.

The pattern is always the same. People see prices rise, they fear missing out, and they buy. Then, one day, the bubble bursts.
