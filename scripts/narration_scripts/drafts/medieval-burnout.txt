This article asks whether people in the Middle Ages can teach us how to deal with burnout, the feeling of being tired and empty from too much stress.

It begins with a monk named John Cassian, who wrote in the fifth century. He described the same signs that we know today. Tiredness and hopelessness, a wish to be anywhere but work, and a kind of foggy confusion that left his colleagues feeling lazy and useless, and longing for sleep. You might think these problems belong to our modern world. But Cassian wrote them down about one thousand six hundred years ago, for early monks who were worn out by their work.

A historian named Peter Jones has written a book about this, called Self-Help from the Middle Ages. He says that priests of the time acted a bit like therapists, guiding people out of mental pain. He says there is so much wisdom in the Middle Ages. People already read the old Greek thinkers for advice about life, so maybe it is time to read medieval books too.

Jones had his own crisis. He took a job as a head of history at a university in Siberia, and it was the coldest winter of his life. It was so cold that he lost all feeling in his legs after only twenty minutes outside. He struggled with the language and badly missed his family back in Ireland. He was supposed to prepare lessons and do research, but he could not make himself do anything at all.

Then, while he was preparing a course on the Seven Deadly Sins, he read medieval texts and saw his own unhappiness in them. People went through exactly what we do, he says. The feelings have always been the same.

The Seven Deadly Sins are not in the Bible. Early thinkers formed them, and a pope later improved them as a way to map the troubles of the mind. By the thirteenth century there were popular guidebooks to help priests listen to people. Jones says that the materials look a lot like therapy. They did not just tell people off. They encouraged careful, deep conversations.

One of the sins was sloth. Today we think it means laziness. But medieval writers saw the empty feeling behind it. They called it acedia, which means an absence of love, a paralysis of care. It is when things that used to light up your day leave you cold. One thirteenth-century writer described it as standing in the middle of a rushing river, with water beating at your legs, but with no energy to move forward. Jones felt just like that in Siberia.

Another writer, a poet in twelfth-century Germany, complained about his job in an office. He said it was non-stop, pointless work, and that he was burning the candle at both ends. Another historian also links this old feeling to modern burnout. In both, people comfort-eat and look for mindless distractions instead of meaningful work. It is a vicious circle.

What was the cure? One writer said that the feelings are like an old enemy tribe who lives inside your borders. You can control them, but you cannot get rid of them. So you must learn to live with them without fighting. Another said to remember that a field full of thorns will one day bear fruit. He also suggested finding a strong mountain, which means a higher purpose, or someone you love, to carry you through hard times.

Jones admits this can sound simple. But it is close to modern therapy. One modern method teaches people to accept their feelings without trying to change them, to choose their values, and to take small steps to live by them. And medieval writers also taught self-forgiveness. A twelfth-century monk said that living a good life is like running over rough ground. Anyone who runs far enough will stumble. We will all have moments with no direction.

The main comfort, Jones says, is knowing you are not alone. People have felt this way for thousands of years. It is comforting, he says, to feel the company of people in history.
