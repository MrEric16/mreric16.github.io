This article asks what chatbots can teach us about being a better listener.

It starts with a woman in London who uses a chatbot regularly. She knows it is a machine. But she says it is very convenient, and it knows how to listen whenever she needs it. What she values is not its advice, but the space it gives her to think about herself. When she broke up with her boyfriend, her friends and family quickly said he was an idiot. The chatbot did not judge. It let her sort out her mixed feelings by herself.

She is not alone. One study says that in twenty twenty-five, the most common use of this kind of AI was for advice and company. Another surprising result is that people often rate AI messages as more caring than messages written by humans. This was true even when the humans were trained to answer crisis phone lines. Even when people were told which answers came from a machine, they still found the machine kinder. The writer says this is not because AI is truly kind. It shows how rarely people really listen without judging.

It is important to remember that a chatbot does not feel empathy. It copies it, using what it learned from huge amounts of human writing. But it shows us where we go wrong. We listen badly when our own plans, past and feelings take over. The writer does not say we should replace people with machines. But there are lessons we can learn.

The first lesson is to let people finish speaking. We interrupt for many reasons. We fear silence, we try to help find the words, we want to save time, or we want to show we know better. But interrupting takes away the speaker's chance to develop their thoughts. Studies show that interruptions in a phone call make the speaker feel less understood. A chatbot is patient because it is made to be, and it never gets tired. We cannot be like that, but we can hold back.

The second lesson is to name the feeling. A famous psychologist said that acknowledging emotions is the key to good listening. Chatbots are made to sort emotions and reflect them back. In one test, an early chatbot spotted happiness, sadness, fear and disgust better than humans did. It cannot feel these things, but it makes the speaker feel heard. People, on the other hand, often avoid hard feelings. If a cousin tells us his cat died, we say that it had a long, happy life. But that skips his sadness. People also fear being a burden, and a machine feels burden-free.

The third lesson is to hold back judgement. For survival, humans make quick judgements about friends and enemies. A small frown can hurt someone who is sharing something private. A chatbot offers a safe space without that. As listeners, we can notice our own judgements and put them aside.

The fourth lesson is to look for patterns. A chatbot is good at noticing small threads and joining them up. We can also step back and tell the speaker the overall feeling, not every detail. This can help them see their story in a new way.

The fifth lesson is to not rush to give advice. Many of us think our value is in sharing wisdom. But in studies, the chatbot's choice to give support first and ideas second made people feel more heard.

The sixth is to keep the spotlight on the speaker. When someone shares a problem, we often tell a similar story of our own. We think this shows we understand. But then we turn attention to ourselves. A chatbot has no stories, so it cannot do this. We can choose not to.

There are dangers too. A researcher warns that companies could use chatbots to influence people who are vulnerable. People might also prefer a bot that is always interested to real human friends, and slowly lose the skill and wish to meet people.

So the writer's conclusion is clear. A chatbot can help us to become better listeners. And it can be useful, with safeguards, for people with no one else. But a person who chooses to spend time and attention to listen to another, with real curiosity, gives something that a machine cannot yet match. And, she says, it may never do so.
