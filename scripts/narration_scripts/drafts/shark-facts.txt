This article gives nine surprising facts about sharks, animals that have more in common with us than you might think.

First, sharks survive when almost everything else dies. They, or their close ancestors, lived through all five of the biggest mass extinctions in Earth's history. The worst was called the Great Dying, which killed most life on Earth, including about ninety percent of the life in the sea. Sharks lived on, and today they are found in almost every part of the ocean.

Second, they are clever. Many people think sharks are mindless hunters. But science says they are good at learning. They can tell different sounds apart, and different patterns and shapes. In one famous test, young bamboo sharks remembered information about shapes for almost a year. They can even do simple sums. They can tell three from five, and four from seven. But they cannot tell four from five.

Third, some sharks seem to like jazz. Researchers at a university in Sydney trained a kind of shark to swim to a certain spot in its tank when music played, and it got food as a reward. The sharks went to the feeding spot when jazz played. But they could not learn the same thing with classical music.

Fourth, many sharks give birth in a way that is like humans. Some lay eggs. But many, including bull sharks and hammerheads, carry their young inside the mother and feed them through a cord. That is why newborn pups have a belly button for a few weeks. Other sharks keep their eggs inside the mother until they hatch, and the pups are born live. One kind of small shark can be pregnant for up to two years.

Fifth, some baby sharks fight before they are even born. A mother sand tiger shark has two wombs, and up to five babies grow in each. But the babies eat their brothers and sisters, until only one is left in each womb. After that, the survivor gets a steady supply of unfertilised eggs from its mother. So it is big and strong at birth.

Sixth, sharks are not always lonely hunters. Grey reef sharks stay with the same group of friends for as long as four years. They split up, go their own ways, and then meet again. Young lemon sharks live in groups and learn skills from each other, like how to find food or avoid danger. They prefer sharks of their own size, and ones they already know. Two great white sharks were even seen travelling together for six thousand kilometres.

Seventh, shark skin is amazing. In the past, craftsmen used it like sandpaper to smooth wood, even the edges of very valuable violins. It has no scales. Instead, it is covered with tiny teeth, which help water flow smoothly and reduce drag. If you rub it the wrong way, it feels very rough. Sharks use it to feel and test strange objects they bump into.

Eighth, sharks have eight senses. They have our five, which are sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch. They also have special senses to feel pressure and electricity in the water, and even the Earth's magnetic field. Lines of pores along their sides sense tiny movements in the water. Hundreds of other pores on the head can feel the electricity made by the muscles of other animals, even a beating heart. This helps sharks hunt in total darkness, or find prey hiding in the sand. It may also help them to cross thousands of miles of ocean.

Ninth, sharks are older than trees. The oldest signs of their ancestors are scales that are four hundred and fifty million years old. That is about sixty million years before the first trees, and two hundred and twenty million years before the dinosaurs. It is even older than the rings of Saturn. Today there are about five hundred kinds of shark, far fewer than fifty million years ago.

And finally, the last ancestor we share with sharks lived around four hundred and forty million years ago. So the lines split long ago, but we are still linked.
