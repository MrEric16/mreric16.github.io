This article is about phone apps that scan the barcode on food and tell you at once whether it is healthy.

Picture a mother in a French supermarket. In one hand she holds a packet of biscuits. In the other hand she holds her phone. She scans the barcode, and the screen turns red. The score is zero out of one hundred. The app says the biscuits contain a lot of sugar and unhealthy fat, and it also finds four additives. Additives are chemicals added to food to keep it fresh or to change its colour or taste. One of them is marked as a health risk. Her twelve-year-old son loves these biscuits, but he now hates shopping with her, because every product takes so long to check.

The app is called Yuka. A group of friends started it in France in two thousand and fifteen. You scan a product, and you get a colour. Green means good, yellow means it could be better, and red means bad. If the colour is red, the app suggests a healthier choice. It works for cosmetics and soap too, not only food. Its database has millions of products, and more are added every day. Today it has tens of millions of users in many countries. The biggest group of users is in the United States, followed by France and the United Kingdom.

Yuka is only one part of a bigger movement. A French programmer created a free website called Open Food Facts. It is built by volunteers, and it now holds information about millions of food products from around the world. There is also a food label called Nutri-Score, made by a French researcher. It gives each product a letter, so that shoppers can see at a glance if it is a good choice. The researcher says he wanted something simpler than a traffic light system. Many big food companies use it. Some companies do not, especially when their products would get a bad score.

This is where the apps help. They show you the information even when the company would rather you did not look. They also point out additives, which are often a sign that a food is heavily processed.

But there are limits. One food policy expert says that most people do not have the time or energy to study every food label. They just buy what they always buy. And the researcher who made Nutri-Score admits that the apps mostly reach people who are already careful about their health, not the people who need help the most. Also, healthier choices can cost more money. The mother in the story says she now buys more organic food, and it is more expensive.

So these apps are useful, but they are not magic. They are a tool, and the real choice is still yours.
