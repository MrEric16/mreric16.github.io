This article is about how scientists look for life on other planets, when we do not know what that life might look like.

We know of only one example of life in the universe, and that is life on Earth. But what if life can form in other ways? Scientists who study this are called astrobiologists. The writer is an astronomer who has written a lot about the subject. He thinks the most common alien life is probably tiny, made of single cells, because those form more easily than big animals.

Since the first planet around another star was found in nineteen ninety-five, more than five thousand have been discovered. Many are small and rocky, like Earth. Some are in the habitable zone. This is the range of distances from a star where a planet could have liquid water on its surface, which life as we know it needs. Looking at what we have found so far, there could be three hundred million places in our galaxy where life might start. These include planets and also moons.

But there is a big problem. Scientists do not agree on what life is. It seems easy, because we know life when we see it, whether it is a bird or a tiny creature in a drop of water. But a full definition may not even be possible. NASA says life is a self-sustaining chemical reaction that can go through Darwinian evolution. This means living things change over time, and the ones that fit their surroundings best survive.

Exoplanets are far away, and hundreds of millions of times fainter than their stars. So studying them is hard. Astronomers use a method called spectroscopy, which reads light to find out what a planet's air is made of. They might find oxygen. On Earth, tiny blue-green algae made oxygen billions of years ago. They might also find signs of chlorophyll, which suggests plants.

However, the NASA definition leaves questions. Is Darwinian evolution the same everywhere? What chemistry can lead to life away from Earth? All life on Earth, from a mushroom spore to a blue whale, came from one tiny ancestor about four billion years ago. The chemistry in every living thing here is similar. It might be the same everywhere. It might also be very different.

In October two thousand twenty-four, a group of scientists met to think about this. They wanted to understand what makes order in the universe, whether living or not. Two researchers said that groups of chemicals or minerals can change over time, if some forms last longer than others. These systems store more and more information and become more varied and complex. They think there may be a law that covers many kinds of systems, and that evolution in living things is just one example.

In biology, information means the instructions stored in DNA. But be careful. It is wrong to say that animals are more complex than bacteria. Bigger genomes hold more information. But in bacteria, a larger share of the genes is useful, so they seem better designed than the genes of plants or animals.

A universal theory of life does not exist yet. It would need ideas like complexity and information, but it would not depend on DNA or on the kind of cells we know.

What does this mean for the search? All known life needs water. Water is a solvent, a liquid in which chemical reactions can happen. But other liquids might work. Two scientists looked at thousands of molecules and suggested sulfuric acid, ammonia, liquid carbon dioxide and even liquid sulfur. Alien life may not be based on carbon. It might not need a planet at all. It could be so strange that we would not recognise it.

So the lesson is simple. To find alien life, we first have to open our minds about what life could be.
