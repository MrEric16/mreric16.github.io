This article is about a growing travel trend called a "darecation," where people choose holidays that push their bodies and minds to the limit.

For most people, a holiday means a beach, a pool and a lot of sleep. But some travellers want the opposite. They want a challenge that is so hard that they will remember it for the rest of their lives.

Take the story of one woman who joined a marathon in Chile. A marathon is a race of about forty-two kilometres. But this one started on top of the highest volcano in the world, almost seven thousand metres above sea level. The air up there has very little oxygen. The temperature fell to minus thirty degrees, and the wind was very strong. Before the race even began, the runners had to climb for about eleven hours just to get to the starting line. Sixteen people signed up, but only five reached the start. The woman who told the story was moving for almost thirty hours in total. She said it was like a draw between her and the volcano, and that she had never been so close to her limits. Would she try it again? Yes, absolutely.

She is not alone. A popular website for ideas said that searches for adventure holidays went up by seventy-five percent. An insurance company reported a huge rise in people buying cover for sports trips, especially trekking, mountain climbing and running events.

Ultramarathons are a good example. An ultramarathon is any race longer than a normal marathon. One big organiser began with a single race around a mountain in the early two thousands. Now it runs more than sixty races around the world, and the number of runners has more than tripled in a few years. Most of them are not professional athletes. They are normal people with jobs who train very hard.

Why is this happening? Experts give a few reasons. First, training and sports food have improved, so ordinary people can do more than before. Second, many people feel tired of gyms and screens, and they want to feel connected with nature. Some say it also helps their mental health. Third, we see more people on television doing extreme challenges for charity. One expert says that when a famous TV presenter climbed Africa's highest mountain, the phone calls to his company went through the roof. People thought, if he can do it, so can I.

A sports psychologist adds something interesting. In a normal road race, your time is everything. In an ultramarathon, the course changes so much that time matters less. The real prize is the story. You remember how beautiful the route was, what you saw, and what you survived.

So the next time you plan a holiday, ask yourself a question. Do you want to rest, or do you want to find out what you can do?
