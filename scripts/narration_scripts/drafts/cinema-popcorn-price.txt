This article asks a simple question: why does popcorn at the cinema cost so much, and why do cinemas not just lower the price?

Most people have a quick answer. The cinema has you trapped. You cannot bring your own snacks, so the cinema can charge whatever it likes. That answer is partly true. A cinema is a bit like a small shop with no competition inside the building. But it is not the whole story, and the rest of the story is more interesting.

Start with a surprising fact. Cinemas do not earn much money from films. When you buy a ticket, the biggest part of that money goes to the film studio. In many deals, the studio takes up to seventy percent. The studio can also set rules. For example, a big new film must play on several screens for several weeks. If the film is not popular, the cinema is stuck with half-empty rooms. And if the cinema refuses the rules, the studio can say it will not send any more films.

At the same time, the cinema has large costs. It has to pay rent, and it has to heat and cool very big rooms. Every few years there is new technology that it must buy to keep customers happy, like better sound, bigger screens and digital projectors. All of this makes the profit very small. In the article, the profit for a whole cinema is only about four percent of its sales.

So where does the money come from? From food and drink. The studio does not take a share of popcorn sales. Snacks bring in only about one fifth of a cinema's income, but they make around forty percent of its profit. An expert in the article says some cinema owners joke that they left the film business and are now in the popcorn business.

The habit started to change around the nineteen seventies. A small cup of popcorn cost a quarter of a dollar. Then it went to fifty cents, then seventy-five cents, then one dollar, all within a few years. By the eighties, expensive snacks were normal.

Here is another point. If you add up the effect of rising prices over time, cinema tickets are not much more expensive than they were forty years ago. In a way, the expensive popcorn helps to keep the ticket price lower. Each person who buys a giant bucket is helping everybody else to pay less for the film.

Now the second question. If the cinema sold cheaper popcorn, would more people buy it, and would the cinema earn more? Probably not. Economists explain that a few people who pay a very high price can bring in more money than many people who pay a fair price. There is nothing in economics that says the best price has to feel fair.

There is also a clever trick behind it. Different people are willing to pay different amounts for a night at the cinema. Someone on a date, for example, expects to spend more. The cinema cannot ask each customer how much money they have. So it offers something that only people with more money, or more willingness to spend, will buy. Very expensive popcorn does exactly that. Some people end up paying extra for the same film, and nobody is forced.

This idea is used everywhere. A games company may sell a console at a very low price, or even for a loss. Then it earns its money from the games. Someone who buys only one game pays little. Someone who buys twenty games pays a lot. Coupons work in a similar way. Shoppers who do not want the trouble of collecting them pay the full price. Shoppers who do want to save pay less.

It also explains why cinemas sell the same snack in very large sizes. A big bucket makes the customer feel they are getting a deal, while the cinema still earns a high profit on every one. The business is built around that one simple product.

So next time you stand in the cinema queue and feel annoyed by the price, remember this. Popcorn is not only a snack. It is a tool that helps the whole business survive.
