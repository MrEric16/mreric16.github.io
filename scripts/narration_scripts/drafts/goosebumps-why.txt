This article is about goosebumps. We know how they happen. But why they happen is still a bit of a mystery.

You can get goosebumps from many things. A cold wind. Nearly tripping over your own feet. A singer who hits a very high note.

The name is simple. When a goose is plucked, its skin shows small bumps where the feathers were. In humans, the way it works is also easy. At the bottom of every hair, there is a tiny muscle. When the muscle tightens, the hair stands up straight.

So why do we have this? Scientists think it is a gift from our ancestors. Long ago, our ancestors had thick, long hair on their bodies. When they were cold, the hair stood up. It trapped a thin layer of air near the skin. That air kept them warm. We no longer have that thick hair. But the reflex has stayed.

That explains the cold. But what about the goosebumps you get from music? You are not cold then. A researcher studies this. The feeling is called frisson, which is a wave of pleasure on your skin. About two-thirds of people feel it.

The newest idea is that it is part of our fight-or-flight response. This is how the body reacts in a fraction of a second to something unexpected. Think about the sound of a branch snapping in the woods at night. Your body does not wait to think. It releases a chemical called adrenaline. You breathe faster, you sweat, and your heart races. Adrenaline also gives you goosebumps.

Our brains have learned to be very careful, to keep us alive. So the same reaction can happen with music. A good singer learns to use the voice in a way that is a bit like a scream. So when something unexpected happens in a song, such as a very high note or a change in the music, the body reacts as if there was a danger. Then your thinking brain catches up. It tells you to relax, because it is only beautiful music. At that moment, you get a flush of another chemical, which makes you feel happy.

The researcher says it is like evolution rewarding us for working out that there is no danger.
