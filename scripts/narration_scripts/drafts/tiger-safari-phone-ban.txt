This article is about India's new rules for tiger safaris. Mobile phones are being banned in the busiest parts of some tiger reserves, and the reasons say a lot about how we behave as wildlife tourists.

Imagine you are in a jeep in a forest in India, and a tiger walks out of the bushes. You reach for your phone, and try to get both your face and the tiger in one photo. It is the stuff of travel dreams. But it is a scene that India's forests will soon not see. In November twenty twenty-five, the country's highest court made a ruling. It said phones, and the way tourists behave with them, are too dangerous for both people and animals. Visitors must now put their phones in a box before they enter the main tourist areas of a tiger reserve, or set them to silent and keep them in a bag.

Why? In February, a video went viral. It showed a wild tiger in a park in Rajasthan, surrounded by many safari vehicles. It had to pick its way around them to escape into the forest, while tourists snapped photos and shouted just a few metres away. It looked stressed and cornered. These crowded scenes are called safari jams, and they are becoming common.

The rules also say that roads in tiger reserves cannot be used between dusk and dawn, except by emergency vehicles. Night safaris are banned, and building around the reserves is limited.

A journalist says people have become reckless in getting photos with animals. Phones have fallen out of jeeps, and guides have had to jump out to get them back. In one case, a child fell out of a jeep because the mother was taking a selfie. The guide had to jump out and pick up the child, with a tiger only a few feet away.

India has more than three thousand six hundred wild tigers. That is about seventy-five percent of all the wild tigers in the world. Most live in the country's fifty-eight reserves. The tiger is still endangered, but conservation work doubled their number between twenty ten and twenty twenty-two. With more tigers has come more demand for safaris. But many visitors do not understand how wild the animals are. Tigers have caused four hundred and eighteen accidental deaths in India in the last five years.

The head of a safari company says phones changed behaviour at every level. Drivers send messages to each other, so sightings spread fast, and more jeeps arrive. Photos tagged with a location make things worse. A spot becomes known as the place where the tiger has her cubs, and everyone goes there. But such areas must be left alone as much as possible. He says that if we are not sensitive to tigers, they will cease to be, and with no tigers there will be no tiger tourism.

The new law also supports sustainable tourism, like homestays and community-run places, and not mass tourism. Businesses have been given three to six months to change. An expert says that the principle is simple. Conservation comes first. Tourist companies should offer good daytime experiences that are not only about seeing a tiger, and visitors should expect slower, calmer trips.

India is not the only country to act. In Kenya, after upsetting video of tourists blocking the path of the great wildebeest migration, the government made new standards for tour companies. In Svalbard, wildlife cruises must now stay between three hundred and five hundred metres from polar bears. And in Sri Lanka, tour operators want stricter rules because of crowded parks.

Some say the problem is bigger than phones. A guide in Kenya says that photographers with big lenses, pushing to get a better angle, can be worse than someone quietly using a phone. The real problem, he says, is what guests expect. If they think a safari is about getting closer at any cost, the guide is under huge pressure. He suggests that visitors should understand their responsibility, and maybe even make a pledge.

If you plan a safari, the article suggests asking whether the company has a recognised sustainability certificate, choosing tours linked to conservation, and using guides and lodges that belong to the local community, so the profits stay there.

A travel writer says tourists should stop chasing tigers and enjoy the whole forest, with its landscapes and birds. That may mean fewer guaranteed sightings. But perhaps that is the point. The privilege of a safari is not getting the shot, but simply being there.
