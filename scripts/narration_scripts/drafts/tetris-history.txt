This article tells the story of Tetris. It is a simple game with a very strange history. It includes spies, secret trips and a big fight over who owned it.

Let's go back to the year nineteen eighty-four, in Moscow. A young computer engineer worked in a government science institute. He was making a small program on a huge old computer. It could only show letters, not pictures. On the screen, a few shapes fell down slowly. The engineer said the game was not complicated. It had no score and no levels. But he started to play, and he could not stop.

He made the game better with help from a colleague and a sixteen-year-old boy. The boy moved the game to a more common type of computer, one that could show colours. Then they shared it with friends. Slowly, it spread. Someone took a copy out of the country, into Hungary. From there it travelled across Europe, from computer to computer, like a virus.

Here is where the story gets messy. In the Soviet Union, private business was not allowed. So the game really belonged to the state. But some companies in Britain started to sell it anyway. They did not have a proper deal. When the Soviet company in charge found out, it was angry. And then a number of companies started to fight for the rights. One company sold the rights to a second company. A second sold them again to a third. And nobody was sure who owned what.

One man understood how special the game was. He was a game designer who saw it at a show in Las Vegas. He wanted to get the rights to a version for a new handheld machine, the Game Boy. So he flew to Moscow, without the right kind of visa. That was dangerous. He was questioned for two hours by officials from the secret police, lawyers and businessmen. When he arrived, two other men were already in the city, trying to get the same rights. One of them even wrote to the leader of the Soviet Union. But the designer won the deal.

There was a legal fight afterwards. Another company had made its own version for a home game console. A court said that company did not have the right to sell it. All the remaining copies were taken off the shelves. Today, only about one hundred thousand copies exist. Collectors love them. An even rarer version was made by a different company, and was cancelled. Only a few copies exist. One of them was once offered for one million dollars.

And the Game Boy? The game became the machine's big hit. It came free in the box in nineteen eighty-nine. It was perfect for the small grey screen and for short, intense games. It changed handheld gaming for ever.

The music is famous too. The tune is an old Russian folk song. In the early nineteen-nineties, a dance version of it reached number two in the British charts. The creator said it was a bit embarrassing, because when children hear the tune, they just shout the name of the game.

Why is it so addictive? A psychologist has an answer. Our brains like unfinished jobs. We keep them in our minds until they are done. In Tetris, every move can finish a line, but it can also create new unfinished work. That chain can go on for hours. It feels like scratching an itch.

Some players even see falling shapes when they close their eyes. People call this the Tetris effect. And a mathematician once showed that, in the old version, you must always lose in the end. A long enough game gives you too many awkward pieces. But newer versions deal the shapes differently, so a player could in theory go on for ever.

About one billion people have played the game. It all started with one engineer who could not stop playing his own program.
