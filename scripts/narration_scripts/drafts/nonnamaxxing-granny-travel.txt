This article is about a new travel trend where young people book trips to learn from grandmothers around the world.

Imagine you are on holiday in a big city. Instead of a normal museum tour, you spend the afternoon in an older woman's kitchen. She teaches you to cook, tells you stories, and laughs when you make mistakes. More and more young travellers want exactly this.

The trend has a funny name, "nonnamaxxing." "Nonna" is the Italian word for grandmother. The idea is simple. You choose activities led by older women, because they feel warm, calm and honest. On social media, videos about grandmother-style hobbies like baking and crafts have been watched hundreds of millions of times. Now that love for "granny life" is moving into real travel plans.

One travel company asked young people from four rich countries about this. Around three out of four said they would book a holiday activity inspired by grandmothers. Many of them said they feel stressed in daily life, and spending time with older people helps them relax.

The writer of the article tried it herself. In Bangkok, she took a beginner course in Thai massage. Almost all of her teachers were older women. They were strict when they needed to be, and they joked with the students. When she got nervous in the final exam and started to cry, her teacher simply gave her a big hug. At the end of the course she got a certificate, and her teacher gave her a thumbs up. She says she felt proud, as if she had pleased her own grandmother.

Another traveller, from Los Angeles, took a pasta class in an Italian grandmother's home near Rome. Her teacher spoke almost no English. But they cooked together, shared snacks, and understood each other with smiles and gestures. She said it felt less like a lesson and more like being welcomed into a family.

There are many other options. You can bake French cakes in Paris, learn to make rice snacks in Tokyo, or taste a traditional dinner in a family home in Budapest. In India, you can learn cooking from a grandmother who uses ideas from old healing traditions. In Australia, an Aboriginal elder shares knowledge about native foods.

Why does it work so well? One expert in the article says this is good for both sides. Young people get skills, comfort and confidence. And older people also gain something. Teaching younger people makes them feel useful, and it can improve their mood and health. A woman in Ireland who teaches visitors to bake brown bread says she loves seeing guests relax in her kitchen. She also notes that many young people enjoy a few hours without their phones.

There is another benefit. Old recipes, skills and customs are passed from one generation to the next. If nobody asks for them, they can slowly disappear. A tourist who sits down to learn them helps keep them alive.

So next time you plan a trip, think about this. The best guide to a new place may not be a book. It may be somebody's grandmother.
