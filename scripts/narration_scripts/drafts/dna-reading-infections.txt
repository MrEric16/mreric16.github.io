This article is about a new kind of medical test that reads the DNA of germs, and how it is already saving lives.

At the end of December twenty nineteen, doctors in China looked at samples from patients with a strange, severe lung illness. All had been at the same market in the city of Wuhan. But the tests showed nothing. The genetic material did not match any germ that was known.

So the doctors used a new method called metagenomic testing. Instead of looking for germs that we already know, it finds everything that is in the sample, known or unknown. It showed that the germ looked a lot like the coronavirus family, which had caused serious illnesses before. The scientists had just discovered Covid nineteen. One expert called it a game changer. Knowing the germ made it possible to build tests quickly, and to start the search for medicines and vaccines.

This is the best known use so far. But now hospitals use it more and more. Metagenomics means studying all the DNA and RNA in a place. First, you take genetic material from a sample. Then machines read the genetic code of everything in it. Computers compare the results with large databases, to find out exactly which bacteria or viruses are present.

Why does it matter? Imagine a patient who arrives at hospital confused, sleepy, with a fever, headache and weakness. This could be a very dangerous infection of the brain or spine. The best treatment depends on the cause. It could be a virus, a bacterium, or the body's own defences attacking the brain. There is little time and few tests, so doctors often have to guess. Often they treat for both infection and an immune problem, which is strange, because the second treatment weakens the immune system.

A professor in California built a test for the fluid around the brain and spine. In one case, a woman was said to have meningitis and liver failure. She was waiting for a liver transplant. The test showed she actually had hepatitis E, a virus that doctors in rich countries rarely check for. She was given a medicine against viruses and got better. She did not need the transplant.

Until recently, there was a problem. The test took days, or even a week. A patient might die before the answer came. Now machines can read DNA in one or two hours, and artificial intelligence can search big sets of data for patterns. This makes the test much easier to use.

It matters most for people with weak immune systems, such as transplant patients, people having cancer treatment, and people with immune diseases. For them, any infection can quickly become deadly. Studies suggest that if they get sepsis, their risk of dying in hospital is twenty-three percent higher. But it is hard to find the cause fast. Pneumonia or sepsis can be caused by hundreds of different germs, so doctors send test after test. As the professor says, it can be a race against time.

In Singapore, a researcher is testing whether AI can make the process faster and more precise. The data is full of noise. It includes harmless viruses that many people carry, and germs from the lab that fall into the sample by accident. A patient may also have several infections, and only one is making them ill. AI can clean the data. It can even look at how the patient's genes are working, to see whether the body is fighting a germ or living peacefully with it. This allows doctors to give the best treatment for each person.

There is more evidence. Last year, a study in two London hospitals used the test on patients with chest infections. It found germs that normal tests miss in thirty percent of cases. In one case, a patient avoided a big operation, because the problem was tuberculosis and not cancer, as doctors thought. The test also found a virus months before official health warnings.

Labs in Singapore now also test sewage and air, using AI to look for new viruses. This could give an early warning of the next pandemic. In Italy, traces of Covid nineteen were found in sewage months before the first official cases.
