This article tells how McDonald's started. It is not what you might think. The world's biggest fast-food company began as a slow barbecue restaurant.

Two brothers opened the first one in nineteen forty, in California. It was a small drive-in near a famous road. They cooked meat slowly for hours in a pit, using wood chips from another state. There were no tables inside, and only a few stools. Young women brought food to the cars. It did well. Soon, it earned over two hundred thousand dollars a year.

After the Second World War, there were more drive-ins in town. The brothers made a surprising discovery. Eighty percent of their sales were hamburgers. One brother said that the more they pushed the barbecue, the more hamburgers they sold.

So they made a big change. They closed for three months. They turned the place into a self-service restaurant, where customers ordered at the window. They let their twenty waitresses go. They replaced plates and cutlery with paper, so they did not need to wash dishes. They cut the menu to nine things. These were hamburgers, cheeseburgers, three soft drinks in one size, milk, coffee, crisps and pie.

Their idea was speed, low prices and large numbers. They copied the car factory idea, where each worker does one job. They called it the Speedee Service System. The kitchen was made like a factory. Each of the twelve workers had a special task. Much of the food was prepared ahead. Every burger came the same way, with ketchup, mustard, onions and two pickles. If you wanted it your way, you had to wait. One brother said that if you offer a choice, you are dead, because the speed is gone.

At first it was hard. The sacked waitresses even laughed at the brothers. But when they replaced crisps with french fries and added thick milkshakes, things took off. Families and businessmen loved the cheap fifteen-cent hamburgers. By the early nineteen-fifties, they earned three hundred and fifty thousand dollars a year, and their profits had doubled. They had already opened a few more in California and Arizona.

Then, in nineteen fifty-four, a salesman visited. He sold milkshake machines. He could not understand why one restaurant needed eight of his machines, which could make forty-eight shakes at once. When he saw the place, he understood. He saw the possibilities, and he became a buyer. He bought the right to open McDonald's restaurants across the country. In nineteen fifty-five, he opened his own in Illinois.

But the relationship was bad. He wanted to move fast. The brothers were careful. They wanted him to write for permission before every change. In nineteen sixty-one, he bought the whole company for two point seven million dollars. The name stayed. But the face of the company was now the salesman. His picture was put on the walls of many restaurants.

The brothers kept their first restaurant, and renamed it. The salesman was angry. So he opened a new McDonald's nearby, and in time it ended their business.

The first restaurant was torn down in the nineteen-seventies. But it shows that a big idea can start small, and often does not give the credit to the people who first had it.
