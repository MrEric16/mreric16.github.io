This article is about a surprising discovery. Some artificial intelligence can be tricked by the same optical illusions as humans. And this tells us something about our own brains.

First, an example. When the Moon is low on the horizon, it looks bigger than when it is high in the sky. But its size has not changed. Illusions like this show that we do not always see the world as it is. People often call them mistakes. But they also show us the clever shortcuts our brains use. Our brains cannot handle every detail of a busy scene. So they pick only what we need.

Computers are different. They are very good at details. They can see patterns and small marks that we miss. That is why they are good at finding early signs of disease in medical scans. But some of these systems, called deep neural networks, can also be fooled by the same tricks as us. A scientist in Japan says this is useful. Experiments on the human brain raise serious questions about ethics. With a computer model, there are no such limits.

Why do we see illusions? There are many theories, but usually no clear answer. One clue comes from a man who lost his sight as a small child, and got it back in his forties. Some shape illusions did not fool him. But illusions of movement did. This may mean that our sense of motion is stronger than our sense of shape. Perhaps we learn to see motion earlier, as babies.

The research team studied a famous illusion called rotating snakes. It is a still picture of colourful circles, but it looks as if the circles are turning. They used a computer model that works on a leading idea about vision. The idea is that our brain does not only react to what the eyes see. First, it guesses what it expects to see, based on past experience. Then it checks the differences. This makes seeing faster.

The model learned to guess the next frame in a video. It was trained on videos of nature, taken by cameras worn on the head. It was never shown an optical illusion. After about a million frames, it had learned some rules about how the visual world works. Then they showed it the rotating snakes picture. It was fooled, just like us. And when they showed it a version that does not fool humans, it was not fooled. This supports the idea that our brains work by making predictions.

But there was also a difference. When we look at one of the circles, it seems to stop. The other circles, at the edge of our view, keep turning. The model saw all the circles moving at once. This is because it has no way of paying attention to one spot.

So computers can copy parts of our vision. But no model can see all the illusions that humans do. The scientist says that a chatbot may talk like a human, but it works very differently inside.

Another researcher combined AI with ideas from quantum physics. His system saw two pictures in one drawing, such as a vase or two faces, and it switched between them over time, just like people. It even switched at a similar speed.

Astronauts see illusions differently too. After three months in space, they see two versions of a cube equally often. On Earth, they see one more than the other. This may be because we use gravity to judge depth.

So, with all the wonders in the universe, astronauts will want to be sure they can trust their eyes.
