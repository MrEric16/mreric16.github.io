This article explains why some places that won a famous world heritage title now want to give it back.

You may know the idea. An international organisation connected to the United Nations makes a list of places that are important to all of humanity. It includes famous sites like Machu Picchu and the Great Wall of China, and also quiet little places that few people have heard of. The list began with twelve sites in the late nineteen seventies. Today it has well over one thousand sites in about one hundred and seventy countries.

The list was created after the Second World War. The aim was to protect special places from war, from fast building work, and from damage by industry. Getting on the list can bring money for protection. There are real success stories. One famous temple complex in Cambodia was badly damaged by war and thieves, and years of careful repair helped to save it. A large coral reef in Belize was moved off a danger list after the country brought in stronger protection.

So why would anyone want to leave? The answer is tourists.

Take a tiny village in the mountains of Slovakia. About twenty people live there all year, in forty-five colourful wooden houses around an old bell tower. It joined the list in nineteen ninety-three. Since then, more than one hundred thousand visitors come every year. Some of the people who live there now say the title has brought more problems than benefits, and they would like to be removed.

An expert in the article says one thing always happens after a place is listed: more visitors arrive. He compares the title to the star ratings in a guidebook. If a place gets the stars, people want to see it. And today social media makes this even faster. In the past, tourists followed guidebooks. Now they follow other tourists, and one popular video can send thousands of people to the same spot.

Big cities feel this too. Venice became a heritage site in the late eighties. It is now one of the most crowded places in Europe, and many local people are moving away because life there has become too expensive. In a Chinese town called Lijiang, the old centre turned into a maze of guesthouses and souvenir shops after it was listed. In Marrakesh, in Morocco, more tourists and foreign money have pushed up house prices.

Researchers have a name for this. They call it "museumification." It means that a living neighbourhood slowly becomes like a museum, built for visitors instead of for the people who live in it.

There is also an argument about what counts as "authentic." One group may say a building has been protected well. Another group may say it looks fake. And the rules say modern changes are allowed, but they must not destroy what made the place special. That can be hard when the residents just need better homes and roads.

The organisation says it now understands that tourism has changed a lot in the last ten or fifteen years, and it is working on new advice for sites. The lesson is clear. Protecting a place is not only about keeping old walls standing. It is also about the people who live there.
