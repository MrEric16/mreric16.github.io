This article is about a project that is trying to record the world's languages before they disappear.

Every two weeks, a language dies when its last speaker dies. It begins in a hall in New York, on the border of Brooklyn and Queens. It looks like a place from the nineteen-forties. On the walls are photos of women who took part in a beauty contest. Years ago, you had to speak the language to take part. Now, some years have only one contestant.

The language is called Gottscheerisch. It comes from a small area of Slovenia, and it is about six hundred years old. A man named Alfred, aged ninety-two, is one of only a few thousand people who still speak it. Every Christmas, he leads a church service in it that few people understand. He says it is like a musician who suddenly cannot use his fingers. We are still alive, he says, but can only remember.

He and his sister agreed to be filmed by a young man named Daniel, who is twenty-six. Daniel is a co-founder of a non-profit called Wikitongues. In New York, people speak about eight hundred languages. That is more than ten percent of all the languages in the world, which number about seven thousand. In twenty fourteen, Daniel and a friend started a plan to make the first public archive of every language. They have already recorded more than three hundred and fifty, and hope to reach one thousand.

Why does it matter? Daniel says that when we lose a language, we lose a lot of possible art, music, books and stories. He asks, would the great Spanish writer have written the same stories in another language?

The numbers are worrying. Between nineteen-fifty and twenty-ten, two hundred and thirty languages died out. Today a third of all languages have fewer than a thousand speakers. By the next century, between half and nine in ten of all languages may disappear.

Sometimes, a language can come back. Hebrew was not spoken in daily life for a very long time, and is now alive again. Catalan grew strong again in the nineteen-seventies. And a Native American language in Oklahoma started to be learned again by students in two thousand and one, more than forty years after its last native speaker had died. The internet helps too. It connects speakers of rare languages, and even texting has helped languages that have no writing.

Wikitongues cannot find every speaker alone. So it uses volunteers in forty countries. They film speakers talking about the past, the present and the future, with questions about childhood, love and hopes. One volunteer recorded a language in Vanuatu that scientists had never studied. Another found a speaker of Ainu, a language in Japan that is not related to any other known language.

Other projects exist too. One builds talking dictionaries with words, sounds and pictures. You can download an app to learn Tuvan, a language of Siberia. Wikitongues' collection will be kept at the Library of Congress. They are also building an app so people can make their own dictionaries with text, sound and video.

But time is short. Recently, one of the last two speakers of a Saami dialect died just before his recording session. They think that about five hundred languages could be lost in the next five years.

Why do languages die? Today, it is less about force and more about life becoming difficult. People move to cities or away from the coast, and they join new communities with new languages. An expert called this a cancer, not a gunshot.

Back in the hall, Alfred and his sister laugh and sing in their language. The sister wrote the first English dictionary of it, in nineteen ninety-four. She says that one day families will say, our family spoke, what was it?
