This article is about inventions that nobody planned. Many things we use every day were found by mistake. Some scientists were trying to do something else. Then something went wrong, and it turned out to be better than the plan.

Let's start with a famous quote. People say it came from a very famous scientist. It says that if we knew what we were doing, it would not be called research. That is exactly what happened with these inventions. Here are six of them.

The first is the X-ray. A German scientist was studying a glass tube with electricity inside it. He noticed a strange glow. So he covered the tube with black paper to block the light. But a nearby screen, with a special chemical on it, still began to glow. He decided that some kind of invisible rays must be passing through. He did not know what they were, so he called them X-rays. The X stands for unknown. Then he took the first X-ray picture of a human hand. It was his wife's hand. When she saw the picture of her own bones, she cried out that she had seen her own death. But the discovery won the very first Nobel Prize in physics in nineteen oh-one. And today, X-rays are a basic part of medicine all over the world.

The second one is the microwave oven. In nineteen forty-six, an engineer was testing a machine that made radar signals. He put his hand in his pocket to get a chocolate and peanut bar. He liked to feed small pieces of it to squirrels during his breaks. But the bar had melted into a sticky mess. That was strange, because this kind of bar does not melt easily. So he tried again, this time with an egg. The egg exploded, and covered his face. Luckily he was not hurt. Next, he tried corn seeds. They turned into popcorn, and he shared it with his colleagues. The first microwave oven went on sale just one year later.

The third is Velcro. Anyone who spends time outdoors knows the burr plant. Its seed cases cling to trousers, shoes, jackets and even hair. A man in Switzerland took his dog for a walk in the woods. When he came back, small seed cases from a plant were stuck all over his clothes and the dog's fur. They were very hard to remove. He looked at one under a microscope. He saw thousands of tiny hooks. That gave him an idea. He made a fastener with two sides. One side has stiff little hooks, like the seed cases. The other side has soft loops, like cloth. After many tests, he found that nylon worked best. The name Velcro comes from two words, velvet and crochet.

The fourth is the sticky note. A chemist at a big company wanted to make a very strong glue for aeroplanes. He failed. His glue was weak, and it did not stay stuck. His first idea was to sell it as a sticky surface for notice boards, so people could pin notes to it. That idea never really took off. For years, nobody knew what to do with it. Then, in nineteen seventy-four, another chemist had a small problem. He sang in a church choir, and his paper bookmarks kept falling out of his song book. He remembered the weak glue. He put some on small pieces of yellow paper from the lab. The notes stayed on the page and left no marks when he took them off. At first, people did not want them. But when the company gave out free samples, nearly everyone who got one ordered more.

The fifth is safety glass. In nineteen oh-three, a French chemist knocked a glass bottle off his desk. It should have broken into many little pieces. But it only cracked, and it kept its shape. The reason was that a thin layer of dried chemical inside the bottle was holding the glass together. Some say he understood the use of this at once. Others say it was only later, when he read about car accidents. In those accidents, windows broke, and sharp glass hurt many people. By nineteen twenty-seven, this kind of glass was used in car windows.

The last one is the coating on your frying pan. In nineteen thirty-eight, a young chemist was working on new gases for fridges. He kept one gas in a small cylinder in the cold. Later he opened it, and no gas came out. But the cylinder was still as heavy as before. Inside, there was a white powder. He tested it. It did not burn easily. Things did not stick to it, and strong acids did not damage it. That is why it was perfect for cooking pots and pans. It was named Teflon. At first it cost too much, so it was used in factories. By nineteen fifty-one, it was also in kitchens.

So what do these stories show us? A good scientist does not only look for what he expects. He also pays attention when something unexpected happens. A glow on a screen, a melted bar, a seed on a dog, a weak glue. Each of these small surprises changed the world.
