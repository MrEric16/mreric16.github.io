This article talks about what space does to the human brain. We know a lot about the body in space. We know much less about the brain.

First, the body. On Earth, gravity pulls on us all the time. In space, that pull is almost gone. So the muscles and bones do less work, and they start to get weaker. After only a few days, bones begin to lose calcium. Faces get puffy, because liquid in the body moves up toward the head. And legs get thinner.

To stay strong, astronauts exercise for about two hours every day. Even then, after six months in space, many astronauts need help to get out of the spacecraft. It can take years for their bones to go back to normal.

Now, the brain. Scientists have not studied it as much. Only a few astronauts have been tested. In one famous study, an astronaut spent a whole year in space while his twin brother stayed on Earth. The astronaut's thinking skills stayed mostly the same in space. But after he came home, they dropped for about six months.

Recently, a team in London put together the results of fifteen studies. They looked at nearly four hundred people. Some were astronauts. Some were volunteers who stayed in bed for weeks, to copy the effects of space.

The team found that the brain really does change. The parts that control movement, balance and the sense of where your body is in space all change. In other words, the brain rewires itself to live without gravity.

This makes sense. Our brains grew up with gravity. Think about picking up a cup of coffee. You do it without thinking, because your brain already knows how heavy the cup is. In space, the cup floats. So the brain has to learn new rules.

This is good news, because it means astronauts can adapt. But it takes time. When the Apollo astronauts walked on the Moon, they often looked wobbly. Their heavy suits were part of the reason. But their brains were also still adjusting to a different kind of pull.

Here is the problem. On a trip to Mars, the crew will spend months in space. Then they must land on a planet where the pull is about one third of Earth's. Their muscles will be strong from exercise. But their brains may be confused. And a confused pilot can be dangerous.

Some people dream of building spaceships that spin, to make a gentle pull. But that would cost far too much. So scientists are testing other ideas. One is to use tiny electric signals to wake up the parts of the brain that sense gravity.

Space is hard on the brain. But it also gives us a new way to study how the brain works.
