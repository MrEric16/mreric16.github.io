This article is about clever homes in Japan. Japanese architects have a special skill. They design homes that are small, but they make life easy and beautiful inside them.

An expert explains why this matters. Much of the world now faces the same questions that Japan has dealt with for many years. How do you live well with less space, fewer resources and more pressure on the environment? He says that a good Japanese home looks simple. But behind that simple look, there are exact choices about size, material, light and the way wood fits together. Good design is not about having more. It is about making what you have work harder.

Here are some of the examples.

The first is a tiny home in Tokyo. It is the home and office of an architect. The land is no bigger than ten square metres. He asks a big question. How much space does a person really need? His home has two tall curved concrete shells that open to the sky. They let winter light in. It has everything needed for bathing, music, food, study and thinking. Every surface has a use. There are shelves cut into the walls, and counters that reach out. He says that his home holds the endless world of small things.

The second is a house with no walls. The space is open from the top to the bottom. Instead of walls, the floors are like building blocks. They float at different heights and fit into each other. They divide the home into areas, and also give storage. A famous artist who drew impossible staircases comes to mind. The people who live there have learned its rhythm. Big windows bring in light, and light screens give privacy to the bathroom and bedroom.

The third is on a corner plot of only thirty square metres. In the West, bigger is often better. In Japan, design looks for ways to live well with less. This house has three floors. On the top, there is a roof terrace. A bathtub is tucked into an angled wall. Tall windows in the roof move the air around. And trees on a raised garden make a small plot into a lovely home.

The fourth is a home planned from the point of view of two cats. In Japan, pets now outnumber children. The architect designed the home for his wife, himself and the cats. In the middle is a spiral staircase with twenty-three levels, made to match a cat's step. From it, like branches from a tree, the bedroom, studio, kitchen and bathroom all lead off. It is playful, and fair for cats and humans.

The last one looks like a pile of toy bricks. It is a family home on a busy shopping street in Tokyo. It is made of stacked cubes, and rises to five floors. There is a small hut at the front, which can be a spare room or a workshop. A spiral staircase goes up through the bedrooms and the living room to a playroom at the top.

All these homes have one thing in common. They do not see small space as a problem. They see it as an idea to work with.
