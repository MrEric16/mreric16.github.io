This article is about a battle. Two sports brands, Nike and Adidas, fought to win the attention of football fans during the World Cup. Their weapons were adverts.

Football is about numbers, like goals and points. For these brands, the numbers are about money. Who sells the most? Who is the most popular? So at the World Cup, both companies did something big.

Each of them made a short film with famous stars. Nike's film is called Rip the Script. It has some of the best-known footballers in the world, and even a famous basketball player. Adidas made a film called Backyard Legends. It shows a local football pitch, a team nobody can beat, and stories that grow into legends. Its cast has young stars, some old heroes, and even a computer-made version of a famous player from England.

These films look like Hollywood movies. They are very expensive. Adidas reportedly spent fifty million pounds on theirs. Nike will not say how much it spent. But it was probably tens of millions too.

So who won? It depends on how you count. On YouTube, Nike was far ahead. Its film had seventy-six million views. Adidas had only about seven million. But in the streets of New York, the picture was different. The two big stores stand opposite each other. The Adidas shop was covered in World Cup signs and football shirts. The Nike shop was thinking more about a basketball team that had just won its title. All around the city, Adidas had pop-up shops, small stands and posters.

Adidas also did well with something else. Its away shirts for some countries became fashion. Young people wore them on the street, not only at the match. A shirt can say who you are, not only who you support. In the number of kits, Adidas also had a small lead over Nike at the tournament. It made fourteen kits. Nike made twelve. Another brand, Puma, made eleven.

An expert in sports brands explains why this matters. He says young fans follow about four different national teams. They also follow individual players. And football now sits next to music, fashion, gaming and design. People talk about old World Cup adverts like old friends. For example, the famous Brazil airport advert from nineteen ninety-eight. They remember them like films.

Today, however, advertising is different. Nobody only watches one long film. People see short pieces on social media. One Nike manager says that stories now travel faster and break into smaller parts. So the film is only the start. The real goal is to give fans something to copy, change and share.

So who really won the battle? The article says it is too early to tell. It was only a first look. But it shows one thing clearly. For these brands, the World Cup is not only a game. It is the biggest stage in the world to sell their idea of football.
