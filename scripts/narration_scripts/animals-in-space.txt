This article asks a simple question. People already know that humans can survive in space. So why do countries still send animals up there?

Let's start at the beginning. In the early days of the space race, countries sent chimpanzees, dogs and rabbits into space. Nobody knew if a living body could survive the speed, the sudden push and the weightless feeling. If the animals survived, scientists thought a human had a fair chance too.

That question has been answered. More than five hundred people have now travelled to space. Space tourism is almost normal. So testing short trips is no longer the big worry. One scientist says that when one country sent a monkey up recently, it was more of a show. There was nothing new to learn.

But that does not mean animals are useless in space. The new questions are about very long trips. For example, a journey to Mars would take about four years. The nearest star outside our solar system is much further. It is four light-years away. A light-year is the distance light travels in one year, about ten trillion kilometres. That is science fiction for now. But scientists want to know how living things react to these conditions.

The biggest danger is radiation. In deep space, a spaceship cannot fully protect you from it. These tiny particles carry so much energy that they can destroy the DNA inside your cells. And scientists cannot copy this danger well on Earth.

So some scientists study a tiny animal called the water bear, or tardigrade. When it is in a hard place, it dries out and goes into a deep sleep. Its body almost stops working. In this state, it can survive radiation, cold and a lack of water. Scientists want to understand how it does that.

Another animal is the nematode. It is a tiny worm. One scientist has studied twenty-four generations of these worms in space. They have a short life, and they have many babies. So scientists can watch a whole life, from start to end, in a short time. This is impossible with humans. And the worms have something in common with us. Their genes that control blood sugar change in space in a similar way to ours.

Animals could also help us eat. If people ever live on another planet, they will need food. A greenhouse with fruit, vegetables and insects that carry pollen might work. But we do not know if the insects will be confused without gravity. We also do not know if plants will grow in the same way. Every living thing on Earth has adapted to the pull of Earth's gravity. So what happens if we change that?

Not every experiment is worth much, though. One scientist says some of them are hard to take seriously. For a price, a company will even send a school project to the space station, and people call it science.

So animals still go to space, but for a new reason. We are not asking if a person can survive a short trip. We are asking if life can survive a very long one.
