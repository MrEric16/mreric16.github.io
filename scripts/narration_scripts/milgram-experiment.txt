This article is about a famous and troubling experiment. It asked a simple question. How far will ordinary people go when someone in charge tells them to do it?

The experiment happened at a university in the United States in the early 1960s. A young teacher at the university ran it. He asked men from the town nearby to join. They were from many different jobs. Each one was paid a few dollars to take part.

The men were told it was a study about memory and learning. They were told it was about whether punishment helps people learn. That was not true. The real study was about obedience.

Here is how it worked. Each man was told he would be either a teacher or a learner. But the draw was fixed. Every real volunteer became the teacher. The learner was an actor.

The teacher sat in front of a machine with thirty switches. Each switch was a stronger electric shock. The first was small. The last was very strong, and it had a danger warning. The teacher read questions to the learner in the next room. Every time the learner gave a wrong answer, the teacher had to press the next switch.

Of course, there were no real shocks. But the teacher did not know that. The actor played a part. First he grunted. Then he cried out. Then he screamed and begged to stop. He said his heart was weak. At the end, he went completely silent.

Many teachers did not want to go on. They were clearly upset. Some begged to stop. But a man in a lab coat stood behind them. He said calm things like, "Please continue," and, "The experiment needs you to go on."

Before the study, the team guessed that almost nobody would go to the end. They thought maybe one or two people in a hundred.

They were very wrong. In the first main study, about two out of every three men went all the way to the strongest shock. Many of them were upset while they did it. But they still did it, because someone in charge told them to.

The team then changed the setup to see what would happen. When the teachers could choose the shock themselves, almost none chose the highest one. So the push from the person in charge really mattered. When two other teachers, also actors, refused to go on, real teachers were much more likely to stop. And when the teacher could see the learner in the same room, fewer people obeyed.

Today, many people say this experiment was wrong to do. The volunteers were tricked. They were put under huge stress. Some left thinking they had really hurt a person. Later studies also found some problems. Sometimes the man in the lab coat did not follow the script. And some volunteers guessed the shocks were fake.

Still, when other scientists tried similar studies in safer ways, they often got similar results. That is why people still talk about it. It shows that good, normal people can do bad things when they are told to. And it shows that one person who says no can help others say no too.
