This article is about a language that is not spoken. It is whistled. It is used on a small island in Spain, and it can send messages for miles.

The island is La Gomera, one of the Canary Islands. It is full of mountains and deep valleys. A person's voice cannot travel far there. But a whistle can. So for hundreds of years, people on the island have used whistles to talk across long distances.

This is not a simple code. It is not like smoke signals, which have only a few meanings agreed in advance. It is a full language. The whistles are the sounds of Spanish. The language is called Silbo Gomero. A whistler can say anything that a speaker of Spanish can say.

How does it work? Try this. Say the sound ee, then the sound oo, and keep your lips still. You will feel your tongue move. For ee, it goes forward and up. For oo, it goes back and down. Now do the same while whistling. Your mouth works like a slide whistle. In this language, the vowels are different heights of sound. The highest is the sound i. The lowest is o. The other vowels are in between.

Consonants are harder. They are not fixed notes. They are movements to or from a vowel. For example, the sounds t, d, r, n and l start from a very high note and drop fast. The sounds p, b, f and g start from a lower note. The sounds are also different in other ways. They can be smooth or short, loud or soft, steady or fading. A person must train to hear all these small differences.

The language was in danger. After the nineteen-fifties, fewer and fewer people used it. But in two thousand nine, the United Nations cultural group put it on a list of the world's oral heritage. Now children on the island learn it in school. And it is coming back.

It is not the only whistled language. They also exist in Turkey, Greece, Mexico and France. Any language can be turned into whistles. Silbo might be older than Spanish. Before the Spanish arrived, people on the island spoke a language from North Africa. They may have whistled that one, and later changed to Spanish.

The writer ends with a joke. Maybe shepherds in England once had a whistled language too. It would be useful, he says, for the places where there is no phone signal.
