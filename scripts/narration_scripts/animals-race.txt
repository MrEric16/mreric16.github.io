This article asks a fun question. Could you win a race against an animal? The answer is more surprising than you might think.

Let's begin with a story. A writer was camping when a black bear walked past. He wondered: could I run away from it? He checked the numbers. The answer was no. A black bear can run at forty-eight kilometres an hour. That is much faster than any person. And by the way, running away from a bear is a bad idea. It can make the bear chase you.

So scientists have measured the top speeds of hundreds of animals. In the past, the methods were rough. One man drove his car next to wild antelope to see how fast they ran. Later, scientists put small animals like rats on treadmills. Today, they use GPS collars. With these, they found that a cheetah in the wild can run at ninety-three kilometres an hour.

Not every test was so high-tech. In one study, scientists wanted to know how fast a spider could run. They gently touched a big hunting spider, and filmed it running next to a ruler. Its usual speed was about seven kilometres an hour. But in short bursts, it reached thirteen. That is not a comforting number.

Now, let's compare. Many people know their time for a five-kilometre run. If you run it in forty minutes, you match the top speed of a small woodland mouse. If you run it in thirty minutes, you match a rat. At twenty-five minutes, you match a house mouse. And at twenty minutes, you match a skunk. Remember, those are the speeds the animals can reach for a short time. You would probably beat them to the finish line.

Here is a surprise. You might think the biggest animals are the fastest. But they are not. Scientists studied about three hundred and fifty land animals. They found that very big animals do not reach their top possible speed. A heavy body takes a long time to get moving. It is like a big plane. It needs a longer runway than a small one. Video game fans know this too. In a famous racing game, the small, light characters speed up fast. The big, heavy ones take much longer. By the time a big animal gets going, it is already tired.

This means you could beat some big animals in a short race. An elephant can run at thirty-five kilometres an hour. But it starts slowly. Over one hundred metres, it would take about fourteen seconds. A well-trained person could beat that.

A former Olympic runner explained why. He said that in his best years, he could run one hundred metres in about eleven point seven seconds. That is slower than a top sprinter, and about the same as a gorilla. But over fifteen hundred metres, his body could hold its speed longer. So he would beat even the fastest sprinter in the world over that distance. Different bodies are built for different races.

Still, the fastest humans would lose to many animals. The fastest man ever ran at about forty-four kilometres an hour. That is faster than a rhino. But a bear is faster. A giraffe is faster too. So is a zebra. And a reindeer can reach eighty kilometres an hour. Santa will be happy.

What about long races? Short sprints use muscles that are strong but get tired quickly. Long runs use muscles that work for a long time. A cheetah is a sprinter. It can hold its top speed for only a few seconds. A hunt usually lasts less than two hundred metres. But an antelope has run eleven kilometres in ten minutes. A hunter only loses a meal. The animal being hunted loses its life. So the hunted animal has more reason to be fast.

Humans are actually better at long distances than at sprints. We have little body hair, and we sweat. This helps us stay cool. Some scientists think this is how early humans hunted. They followed animals for many kilometres, until the animals were too tired to go on.

So could a human win a marathon against an animal? Maybe not. There is a problem. Scientists do not have much data. Animals do not run marathons for fun. In a lab, it would be unfair to force them. And in the wild, they would stop long before the finish line.

Scientists do keep records of travel speeds. This is the pace an animal can keep up for a long time, a bit like our walking. By that measure, the bear is slow. A bear walks slowly when it travels, about two and a half kilometres an hour. Wolves and camels are also slow. But those are walking speeds, not their fastest.

The best animal runners are dogs. Sled dogs in marathon races are more than half an hour faster than the human world record. A trained horse could also beat most people. In Wales, there is a yearly race between humans and horses on a hilly path. The horse usually wins. But sometimes a human wins.

Sled dogs go even further. In a famous race in Alaska, teams cover sixteen hundred kilometres. The fastest team finished in about seven and a half days. Humans who walk and run the same kind of route in Alaska take almost three weeks. So dogs win the very long races too.

For very long races, people have a special power. It is the mind. One runner went around a single city block in New York for more than forty days. A runner says that long races are about being comfortable when it hurts. Maybe animals do not do that.

And what about the squirrel or the badger? Sadly, they are small, and that helps them. They reach top speed in one or two steps. They run at about thirty kilometres an hour. That is faster than most people can sprint.
