This article talks about where everyday English words actually come from — and almost none of them mean what you'd expect once you dig into the history.

Start with disease and medicine, since a surprising number of ordinary words trace back there. "Quarantine" comes from an old Italian phrase for "forty days" — how long ships had to sit offshore in Venice before docking, to check for plague. "Malaria" literally means "bad air" in Italian, from the old belief that swamp fumes caused the disease, long before mosquitoes were ever suspected. "Vaccine" comes from the Latin word for cow, because the very first vaccine used cowpox to build resistance to smallpox. And "obstetric" comes from a Latin word meaning "to stand in front of" — describing someone standing before a baby as it's born.

Then there's a whole group built on old superstition or outdated belief. "Sinister" originally just meant "left side" in Latin, from a time when left-handed people were seen as untrustworthy. "Nice" might be the strangest case in the language — it used to mean "foolish" or "ignorant," drifting for centuries through meanings like "fussy" and "shy" before finally settling on "pleasant." "Gossip" didn't start as rumor at all — it meant "godparent," someone trusted enough to stand beside you at a baptism. And "dunce" comes from the followers of a medieval theologian whose ideas later fell out of fashion — his name became an insult once his thinking was considered old-fashioned.

A big cluster of words are just named after real people. "Boycott" comes from a British land agent in Ireland whose tenants refused to deal with him over unfair rent. "Silhouette" is named after a famously stingy French politician — cheap black paper outlines got stuck with his name as a kind of insult. "Nicotine" comes from the diplomat who introduced tobacco to France. "Saxophone" is named after its actual inventor, Adolphe Sax. "Braille" is named after the blind teenager who invented the reading system. And "gerrymandering" comes from a governor whose oddly shaped voting district looked like a salamander on a map.

Some words were simply invented, by name, by one person. "Robot" comes from a 1920s play, built from a Czech word connected to forced labor. "Pandemonium" was coined by the poet John Milton as the literal capital of hell — only later did it come to mean ordinary chaos. "Chortle" didn't exist until Lewis Carroll smashed "chuckle" and "snort" together in a poem. And "factoid" was coined by a novelist to describe a fake fact that sounds true enough to spread anyway.

Several come from very literal, almost embarrassingly simple origins. "Mortgage" literally means "dead pledge" in Old French — the deal only ends when you pay it off or default, so something "dies" either way. "Window" comes from Old Norse for "wind eye," from back when windows were just holes in a roof. "Companion" comes from Latin for "with bread" — someone you share a meal with. "School" comes from the Greek word for "leisure," because in ancient Athens, spare time was what let people sit around and learn. "Checkmate" likely comes from Persian for "the king has no way out," which fits chess much better than the more popular but shakier "king is dead" theory. And "plumber" comes from the Latin word for lead, because pipes used to be made of it.

A last group covers color and nature words with their own odd stories. "Orange" the color didn't exist as a word in English until the fruit arrived — before that, people just said "yellow-red." "Crimson" likely traces back to an ancient word for "worm," since the dye came from crushing a particular insect. "Daisy" comes from Old English for "day's eye," because the flower opens at dawn and closes at dusk. "Orchid" comes from the Greek word for a part of male anatomy, based on the shape of its roots. "Tulip" comes from the Turkish word for turban, because of the flower's shape. "Dolphin" comes from the Greek word for womb, for reasons nobody's fully explained. And "walrus" has such a disputed origin that J. R. R. Tolkien — who worked on the dictionary before he was a famous author — once listed six competing theories for where it came from.

The pattern running through all of it: words are basically fossils. The sound survives for centuries after the original reason behind it has been completely forgotten.
