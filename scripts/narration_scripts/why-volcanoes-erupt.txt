This article explains what volcanoes are, why they erupt, and how dangerous they can be.

Volcanoes have thrown out melted rock, hot ash and gas since Earth was young, billions of years ago. They have made more than eighty percent of the planet's surface. Over time, wind and rain break down volcanic rock, and this makes very rich soil. That is one reason why whole civilisations have grown near volcanoes.

There are volcanoes on every continent, even Antarctica. About one thousand five hundred are still thought to be active. Each one is different. Some explode, like Mount Saint Helens in nineteen-eighty. Others just pour out rivers of lava, like Kilauea in Hawaii in twenty eighteen. The difference is chemistry. When the melted rock, called magma, is runny, the gas escapes easily and the magma flows down the slopes. When it is thick and sticky, it traps the gas. The pressure builds up until it breaks free with a huge explosion.

Most volcanoes form where the giant plates of Earth's crust meet. These plates move slowly and bump into each other. Sometimes one plate sinks under another. As it goes deeper, the heat and pressure squeeze water out of the rock. The water makes the rock above it melt more easily, and magma forms and rises. Other volcanoes form over a hotspot in the middle of a plate. The hotspot stays in place, but the plate slowly moves over it. This makes a line of islands, and that is how the Hawaiian islands were made.

Volcanoes have different shapes. Tall, steep ones, like Mount Fuji in Japan, are built from many layers of lava and ash. Cinder cones are made of small pieces of solid lava that fall around a vent. Shield volcanoes are wide and gentle, like a dome. Mauna Loa in Hawaii is the biggest active one. Some volcanoes are just long cracks in the ground, with lava flowing out far and wide.

About three quarters of the active volcanoes in the world sit around the Ring of Fire. This is a huge horseshoe, twenty-five thousand miles long, around the Pacific Ocean. But here is an important fact. The volcanoes in the ring are not connected. An eruption in Indonesia does not cause one in Alaska.

Eruptions are dangerous in many ways, not only because of lava. A pyroclastic flow is a fast cloud of hot rocks, ash and poisonous gas. It can move at up to four hundred and fifty miles an hour. One killed the people of Pompeii when Vesuvius erupted in the year seventy-nine. Mudflows, called lahars, can bury towns. And volcanic ash is not soft like ash from a fire. It is made of sharp pieces of rock and glass. It is bad to breathe, it is heavy, and it can make roofs fall in or cut the power.

Scientists watch volcanoes for warning signs. These include small earthquakes, a swelling of the sides, and more gas coming out. But no one can say exactly when a volcano will erupt. Volcanoes do not follow a timetable like a train. So a volcano can never really be overdue.

The deadliest eruption in history was in eighteen fifteen, in Indonesia. It was one of the most powerful ever recorded. Ash shot twenty-eight miles into the sky. About ten thousand people died at once. Then the ash blocked the sun and cooled the world, and the next year was called the year without a summer. Hunger and disease killed about eighty-two thousand more people. Some say the gloomy weather even inspired the story of Frankenstein.

Finally, are there more eruptions today? No. At least a dozen volcanoes erupt on any day, but that is normal. We just hear about them more. As one scientist said, the world is not more volcanically active, we are just more volcanically aware.
