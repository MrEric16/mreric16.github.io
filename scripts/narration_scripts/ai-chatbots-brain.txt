This article asks a serious question. If we let AI chatbots think for us, what happens to our own brains?

It begins with a scientist at a famous university in the United States. She was looking for interns. She noticed that the cover letters were strangely alike. They were long and polished. After a short introduction, many jumped to an odd link with her work. It was clear that the students had used a chatbot to write them. At the same time, she saw that students in her classes forgot things more easily than before. She had a guess that using chatbots was changing how they think.

Scientists call this cognitive offloading. It means giving a mental job to a tool. This is not new. When the internet arrived, people remembered fewer details, because they could search for them. This was called the Google effect. Some say the internet is a kind of outside memory, which leaves our brains free for other tasks. But now we give away even more. Chatbots can write poems, give advice about money and be a companion. And students use them to do their work.

So the scientist ran a test. She asked fifty-four students to write short essays. The topics were open, like loyalty or happiness. She split them into three groups. One group used a chatbot. The second used a search engine, without AI summaries. The third used nothing but their own minds. Their brain waves were measured the whole time.

The results are not yet published in a journal, but they are striking. The group using only their own minds had a brain that was, in her words, on fire. Many parts were active. The search engine group was also quite active, especially in the parts for seeing. But the chatbot group had much less activity. It was reduced by up to fifty-five percent. Their brains did not fall asleep, she says. But the areas for creativity and for processing information were much less active.

Memory was also affected. After the essay, people in the chatbot group could not quote from their own work. Several felt that it did not really belong to them. Teachers said the chatbot essays were very similar, and soulless. One teacher asked if the students had been sitting next to each other.

Other research agrees. Another group of researchers found that some people accept what a chatbot says without checking it, and even let it overrule their own feelings. They call this cognitive surrender. And there is a case in medicine. Doctors who used an AI tool to look for signs of cancer for three months were later worse at finding it without the tool.

What about the long term? Nobody knows yet. But in this study, four months later, the students were asked to write again. This time those who used the chatbot before had to work alone. Their brains showed weaker connections than the students who switched the other way.

Is it all bad? No. Another expert says that chatbots can help us to think. But only if we do not hand over the whole job. She worries that most people do not use them this way. In her own study, university students had to predict things like the price of oil. Most just asked the chatbot and copied the answer. Their brain activity, which shows mental effort, was very low.

So the message is simple. A chatbot can be a good helper. But if you let it do all your thinking, your own mind may get weaker.
