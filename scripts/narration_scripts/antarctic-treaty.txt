This article tells how the world agreed to keep one continent peaceful. It is Antarctica, and the agreement is called the Antarctic Treaty.

It might seem strange. In the 1950s, the United States and the Soviet Union were rivals. It was the time of the Cold War. They did not agree on much. But in 1959, twelve countries met in Washington, D.C. They agreed on something important. Nobody could use Antarctica for war.

Let's go back in time. People have guessed that a southern continent existed since ancient Greece. But nobody saw it until 1820. Around that time, three expeditions saw the mainland within a short period of each other. They were from Britain, the United States, and Russia. Nobody knows who was really first.

Later, in 1911, a Norwegian explorer led the first group to reach the South Pole.

Meanwhile, countries began to claim parts of the land. Between 1908 and 1943, seven countries made claims. They were Argentina, Chile, Australia, the United Kingdom, New Zealand, Norway, and France. The trouble was that three claims overlapped. They belonged to Argentina, Chile, and Britain. That can be dangerous. Many wars in history started over land.

People tried to solve it. Britain asked the three countries to go to an international court, twice. But Argentina and Chile said no. Other plans were suggested. None worked.

Then came an important event. It was called the International Geophysical Year. It started in 1957 and lasted eighteen months. Twelve countries did big science projects in Antarctica. The seven countries with claims joined in, with the United States, the Soviet Union, Japan, Belgium, and South Africa. The project was a great success. Scientists from different countries worked side by side.

So the United States invited all twelve countries to Washington. They met from October to December 1959. And they made the treaty. It started to work in 1961.

What does the treaty say? Antarctica can only be used for peaceful purposes. No military bases are allowed. No weapons can be tested. No nuclear explosions, and no nuclear waste. Scientists have the freedom to do research, and they must share what they find. Each country can visit the others' bases and check them.

The treaty does not take away any country's claim. But it says that nobody can fight over a claim.

Today, the treaty has grown into a larger system. By 2015, fifty-three countries belonged to it. It shows that even rivals can agree, when science brings them together.
