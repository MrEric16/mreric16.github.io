This article talks about how something as heavy as a commercial airplane manages to get off the ground and stay in the air — and the basic idea is actually simpler than most people expect.

Flight comes down to a balance between four forces. Weight pulls the plane down, obviously. Lift pushes it up, fighting against that weight. Thrust pushes the plane forward, and drag pushes back against it, slowing it down. As long as lift beats weight, and thrust beats drag, the plane climbs and moves forward. Change that balance, and the plane does something different — slows down, descends, whatever the new balance calls for.

Lift is the part that confuses people most, but it comes from the wing's shape. A wing is curved on top and flatter underneath. As air rushes over and under the wing, the air moving over the curved top has to travel faster, which lowers the pressure above the wing compared to below it. That pressure difference is what pushes the wing — and the whole plane — upward. The faster the plane moves, the more lift it generates, which is exactly why planes need a long runway to build up speed before takeoff.

Thrust comes from the engines, which burn fuel to blast air backward at high speed — and by simple physics, pushing air backward pushes the plane forward.

Once in the air, the pilot steers using small moving sections built into the wings and tail. Flaps on the wings tilt the plane left or right. A panel on the tail tilts the nose up or down. And a vertical piece on the tail swings the nose left or right, like a rudder on a boat. Small, precise movements of these parts are really all it takes to control something the size of a commercial jet.
