This article talks about a famous idea from game theory called the Prisoner's Dilemma. Once you understand it, you start to see the same pattern everywhere in real life — not just in crime stories.

Here is the classic example. Two people are arrested together. The police put them in separate rooms, so they cannot talk to each other. Each person gets the same offer: confess and blame the other person, or stay silent. There are four possible results. If both stay silent, they both get a light sentence — the police don't have enough proof for more. If both confess, they both get a medium sentence. But if one confesses and the other stays silent, the result is very unequal: the person who confessed goes completely free, while the one who stayed silent gets the worst sentence of all.

Now here is the interesting part. Each person has to decide without knowing what the other one will do. So think about it from one person's side. "If my partner stays silent, what should I do? Confess — then I go free instead of getting a light sentence." "And if my partner confesses, what should I do? Still confess — because staying silent in that case gives me the worst result." So no matter what the other person chooses, confessing is always the better choice for you personally. Both people think this way on their own. Both confess. Both end up with the medium sentence. But if they had simply trusted each other and stayed silent, they both would have gotten the lighter sentence instead. Each person acting in their own interest, in a completely logical way, created a worse result for both of them.

You don't need a police station to see this. Imagine two students who agree to split the work for a group project, but secretly, each one is tempted to do less and let the other one carry it. If both students do their fair share, the project turns out well and both get a good grade with less stress. If both students slack off, the project is weak and both grades suffer. But if only one student slacks off while the other does all the work, the lazy student gets a free ride — a good grade for almost no effort — while the hardworking student gets stuck doing double the work for the same grade. That fear, of being the one who works hard while the other person free-rides, is exactly what can push both students toward doing less — even though full effort from both would have been the better result for everyone.

This same pattern shows up again and again outside of school too. Think about an arms race between two countries. Each country builds more weapons because not building them, while the other side builds up, feels dangerous. But when both countries think this way, they end up with two heavily armed nations — and a more dangerous world than if neither had built up at all. The exact same trap, just much bigger. Or think about two businesses competing on price. Each company lowers its prices to win customers. That seems smart for each company alone. But if every company does it, profits fall for the whole industry, and nobody actually wins the advantage they wanted. Shared resources work the same way too — a shared lake for fishing, or a public field. Each person gets a small personal benefit by taking a bit more. But if everyone thinks this way, the shared resource gets used up, and everyone ends up worse off. This pattern even has its own name: the Tragedy of the Commons.

One thing changes everything, though — how many times people interact. Everything above describes what happens when people only interact once. There is no "next time" to think about. But when the same two people, or the same two countries, or the same two companies, deal with each other again and again, the thinking changes. Betraying someone today can cost you their trust tomorrow — they might simply stop cooperating with you in every future round, as punishment. That changes everything. Being reliable and cooperative today becomes the smarter choice, because the relationship does not end after one round. This is a big reason why long-term relationships — between classmates, coworkers, businesses, or even countries — tend to move toward cooperation over time, even when a single, one-time interaction would push toward betrayal instead.
