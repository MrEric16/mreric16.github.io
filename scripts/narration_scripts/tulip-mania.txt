This article tells the true story of tulip mania. You may know the famous version. Long ago in the Netherlands, people went crazy for tulips. They paid the price of a house for one flower. Then everything crashed, and the country was ruined. But a historian who studied the old records says that most of this story is not true.

First, what is the famous story? It says the craze was in the 1630s. Everyone was involved, from street cleaners to nobles. It says one bulb was traded ten times a day. It says people lost everything and jumped into the canals. And it says the government stopped the trade, but only after it had wrecked the economy.

Now, the real story. The historian worked for years in the old Dutch archives. This is what was found.

Was it crazy to buy tulips? Not really. Tulips were a new luxury. The Netherlands was growing rich, and more people could afford nice things. Tulips were beautiful and rare. It was hard to grow the ones with striped or spotted petals. Rich people who bought tulips often bought paintings, too, or collected things like shells. So a high price was not so strange.

Was it a wild frenzy? No. For most of the time, the trade was quite calm. It happened in small meeting places and in local pubs. It became more and more organised. Towns had companies to grow, buy and sell the flowers, and groups of experts to watch over the trade. And the idea that bulbs were traded again and again is wrong. The historian never found a chain of more than five buyers, and most were much shorter.

Were prices always crazy? No. It is true that the very best tulips cost about five thousand guilders. That was the price of a nice house. But the historian found only thirty-seven people who spent more than three hundred guilders. That is about a year of pay for a skilled craftsman. And almost all of them were rich merchants who could afford it. Most buyers and sellers knew each other, from the same family, church, or street.

What about the crash in February 1637? It did not happen because foolish new buyers came into the market. More likely, people feared there were too many tulips, and that prices could not keep rising. And the losses were mostly on paper. The bulbs were still in the ground. No money would change hands until May or June, when the bulbs could be handed over. So people who lost money had only lost the chance of a profit. And those people could afford it.

What about the people who drowned themselves? The historian found no one. Not a single person went bankrupt because of tulips. The Dutch economy was not hurt at all. And the government did not stop the trade. In fact, it moved slowly and was not sure what to do.

So why do we believe the old story? Because of a few popular writers. After the crash, people wrote funny songs and pamphlets that made fun of the traders. Writers repeated them for years. Finally, a famous book in 1841 told the story again, and it sold very well. Today it still appears in financial websites and popular books.

Some people call new money crazes "tulip mania." But the historian says we should be careful. Tulip mania was an event of its own time, with its own causes. And it was not the foolish story we have been told.
