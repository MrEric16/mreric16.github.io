This article is about the Nuremberg trials. They were some of the most important trials in history.

First, some background. During the Second World War, the Nazi government killed millions of people. About six million of them were Jewish. When the war ended in 1945, the winning countries wanted the people responsible to face justice.

At first, there was a different idea. One leader wanted to simply shoot tens of thousands of German officers. Another leader talked about punishing top Nazis without a trial. But American leaders convinced them to hold real trials. A trial would have to show proof of the crimes. It would also stop people from saying later that the men were punished without evidence.

There were big problems. Nobody had ever held a trial like this. Four countries had to work together: the United States, Britain, France and the Soviet Union. Each one had a different legal system. So they wrote a new set of rules in 1945. It named three kinds of crime: crimes against peace, such as starting a war, war crimes, and crimes against humanity, such as murder and slavery.

They chose the city of Nuremberg in Germany for two reasons. Its courthouse was not badly damaged by the war, and it had a big prison next to it. Also, the Nazis had held their big rallies there. So holding the trials in that city was a symbol that the Nazi government was over.

The first and most famous trial began in November 1945 and ended in October 1946. Twenty-four men were accused. They included top officials and military leaders. The Nazi leader himself was already dead, so he was never tried.

The accused could choose their own lawyers. They had two main defences. One was that these laws did not exist when the acts happened. The other was that this was justice from the winners. The judges still found most of the men guilty. Of those on trial, all but three were guilty. Twelve were given the death sentence, and the others got prison sentences from ten years to life.

The trial also gave us something we still use. Four languages were spoken in the room. So a company built a system where people translated at the same time and sent their words through headphones. This is where live translation started.

After that, there were twelve more trials until 1949. These included doctors, judges, business leaders and army officers.

Even then, some people said the trials were not fair. But most people saw them as a big step. They led to important agreements about human rights and genocide. And they showed that leaders can be held responsible for crimes, even in wartime.
