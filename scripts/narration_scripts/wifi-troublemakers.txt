This article is about wi-fi, and about the strange things that can break it. Some of them are in your own home. One of them might be your lunch.

Wi-fi works with radio waves. These waves carry information through the air. And many things can get in their way. One of the first people to build a large wi-fi network was a professor in nineteen ninety-three. He calls these problems the bad boys of radio.

Let's start with a famous story. For seventeen years, astronomers in Australia were confused by strange radio signals. Some people blamed the Sun. Some thought it was aliens. In the end, they found the real reason. It was the microwave oven in the office kitchen. Every time someone heated lunch, the telescope picked up its energy.

This is not only a problem for telescopes. Many wi-fi networks use a radio signal at a frequency of two point four gigahertz. A microwave oven uses exactly the same frequency to heat your food. A good microwave keeps its waves inside. But an old or damaged one can leak, especially if you open the door too soon. Then your wi-fi can slow down or drop. Today it happens less. Machines are built better, and wi-fi can also use a different frequency, five gigahertz.

Here is a surprise. A fish tank can be a problem. Water takes energy from radio waves. So a big tank between you and your router can make a dead spot in your room. The expert calls this shadowing, and he says it is the biggest wi-fi problem of all. Thick walls made of brick or concrete do the same. Wood and plasterboard are easier for the signal to pass through. The more things in the way, the weaker it gets.

Another problem is reflection. Radio is a kind of light. Like light, it can bounce off flat shiny things, such as a mirror. A big television can do it too. Some buildings also have metal sheets in the walls. If you have a dead spot, imagine a line from you to the router. Ask yourself if a mirror or a screen is in the way.

How can you fix these problems? There are some easy ways. Put the router in the middle of your home, as high as you can. Use a wi-fi extender to boost the signal. Or buy a mesh system, which uses several small devices around your home. Then nobody has to move the fish.

Weather can also be a problem. Normal rain is usually fine. But snow can damage cables or cover satellite dishes. Heat can cause trouble too. And sometimes the problem is just that everyone in your home is watching videos at the same time.

The expert now lives in Alaska, and he helps remote villages to get online. Even there, he says, the problems are very simple. When a snowstorm covers a satellite dish, you may just need a shovel.
