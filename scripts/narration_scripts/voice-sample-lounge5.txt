Today's piece looks at the IKEA effect — the tendency to value something more highly simply because you built it yourself. The term comes from a well-known 2012 psychology study. In one experiment, people who folded paper origami rated their own clumsy attempts almost as valuable as origami folded by an expert — but only if they actually finished the project. When people failed to complete it, that inflated sense of value disappeared completely.

The article points to a few reasons this happens. Part of it is about competence — assembling something yourself proves to you that you're capable, and that feeling attaches itself to the object. Part of it is effort justification: the more work something costs us, the more our minds convince us it was worth it, so the effort doesn't feel wasted. And part of it is simple bias toward ourselves — we tend to think well of our own work, fairly or not.

This shows up everywhere once you start looking for it. IKEA and Build-a-Bear build entire business models on it, charging customers for the privilege of doing part of the labor themselves. Meal-kit companies like HelloFresh sell you pre-measured ingredients instead of a finished meal, for the same reason — you'll rate a dinner higher if you cooked it, even using someone else's recipe and someone else's shopping. Open-source software communities see it too: volunteers defend code they personally wrote long after a better alternative exists. Even in a classroom, this plays out — students often trust their own messy notes over a tidier set someone else wrote, not because their notes are better, but because they made them.

Worth sitting with for a second: the next time you're sure something you made is good, ask whether that's really true, or whether it's just this bias talking.
