This article tells the story of the first alphabet. Every day, you use letters to read and write. But for most of history, writing was much harder.

Before the alphabet, people had other ways to write. In Egypt, they used little pictures called hieroglyphics. In another old land, people pressed a stick into soft clay to make wedge shapes. These systems worked. But there was a big problem. They needed a huge number of symbols. Almost every word had its own symbol. So writing was very hard to learn. Only a small group of trained workers, called scribes, could do it.

Then, sometime between about 1850 and 1700 years before the time of Jesus, something changed. A group of people took some of the Egyptian pictures and used them in a new way. Each picture now stood for one sound in their language. This was a clever idea. You did not need thousands of symbols any more. You only needed a few.

This early writing is often called the first alphabet. It had no vowels. The symbols stood only for consonants. And it was written from right to left.

The idea spread fast. Traders on ships carried it around the sea. They were called the Phoenicians, and they lived in a part of the world that includes today's Lebanon and Syria. Their alphabet had just twenty-two symbols. That was easy enough for ordinary traders to learn and to draw. So writing was no longer only for scribes.

By the 8th century before our time, the Phoenician alphabet had reached Greece. The Greeks changed it. They kept some of the symbols, and they dropped others. But their biggest idea was to add letters for vowels. Now people could read a text and say it correctly, without guessing. Many experts believe this is the moment when the first true alphabet was born.

The Greeks also changed the direction of their writing. At first, they wrote from right to left. Then they tried something odd. One line went right to left, and the next line went left to right. The name for this is a word meaning "turning like oxen," because oxen turn at the end of each row in a field. By about 500 years before our time, the Greeks had decided. They wrote from left to right, as we do today.

The Greek alphabet had many children. One of them was the Latin alphabet, which spread across Europe. Another was the Cyrillic alphabet, which became the base of the modern Russian alphabet.

So the next time you write a word, remember its long history. It began with a few clever people, a few Egyptian pictures, and some busy traders.
