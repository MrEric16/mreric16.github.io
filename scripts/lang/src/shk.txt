INTRO: This is Early Modern English, the language of Shakespeare (about 1590 to 1616), shown in the modern spelling used in school editions. Read each word as you would modern English, then learn the old forms.
th | in "thou", "thee", "thy", "thine" it is the soft "th" of "this" | thou
-est | the ending for "thou": thou lovest, thou hast, thou art | lovest
-eth | the old ending for "he / she / it": he loveth, he hath, he doth | loveth
' | an apostrophe shows a dropped letter: 'tis (it is), o'er (over), e'en (even), ne'er (never) | 'tis
wh- | wherefore means "why", not "where". Whence = from where. Whither = to where | wherefore
-ed | often spoken as a separate syllable in verse: "blessed", "loved" | loved
NOTE: "Thou" is for one person, informally (a friend, a child, a servant, God). "You" is polite, or plural. Shakespeare uses the choice for effect: switching from "you" to "thou" can be an insult or a sign of love.
NOTE: Modern things (phone, wifi, computer, train, bicycle, airport) did not exist. Where there is a period word it is used (coach for bus, hackney for taxi). Where not, the modern noun is kept inside Early Modern grammar, and it is not an attested line.
NOTE: This is a teaching language: Early Modern grammar and vocabulary, not quotations. Real Elizabethan spelling and pronunciation varied. Stress is as in modern English.

## Formal vs informal
> "Thou" is close or lower. "You" is polite. "Sir", "madam" and "my lord" are used for respect.
row: you (informal) | thou
row: you (formal) | you
row: sir (polite) | sir / my lord
ex: how are you? (informal) | How fares it with thee?
ex: how are you? (formal) | How fare you?
ex: thank you, sir | I thank you, sir
ex: excuse me, madam | By your leave, madam
ex: please sit down | Pray you, sit
## Past and future (simple)
> Past: add -ed (laboured), or use the old forms (did, was, went). Future: "shall" is the plain future. "Will" means want, or intend.

## Commands

## Comparing things
> Add -er and -est, or use more and most. Shakespeare may double them: the most unkindest cut. Than uses the subject form: taller than I.

## Moods and wishes
> A wish uses "would": Would I were a bird! A condition uses "an" or "if": An thou wilt, come. After "if" the verb may be in the subjunctive: If it rain.
row: wish | Would I were...
row: if | an / if
row: let us | Let us...
ex: if it rain, I will stay at home | If it rain, I will stay at home
ex: let us go | Let us go

ex: if thou wilt, come | An thou wilt, come
ex: pray you, come in | Pray you, come in
